Accidents, Safety Analysis & Severe Accidents · C-SA-003

Containment Hydrogen Control Strategy — Mixing, Recombination, Ignition and Inerting Compared, and the 10 % Criterion

Managing hydrogen concentration and combustion mode by mixing, monitoring, recombination, deliberate ignition and inerting.

Concept C-SA-003
Definition

Containment hydrogen control is the design and operating strategy that manages hydrogen concentration and combustion mode — by mixing, monitoring, removal (recombination or deliberate ignition) and inerting — so that hydrogen released into containment during an accident cannot damage the containment or essential equipment through deflagration or detonation.

Principle and mechanism

Hydrogen sources in brief

The main source is cladding oxidation (Zr + 2H₂O → ZrO₂ + 2H₂, exothermic). If 1 kg of Zr reacts completely it produces 0.0442 kg of H₂ (about 0.49 m³ at standard conditions). Other sources are radiolysis of water, corrosion of Al and Zn inside containment, and molten core–concrete interaction (MCCI, producing H₂ and CO). In design basis accidents the concern was slow generation such as radiolysis; in a severe accident the generation rate is far higher and exceeds the capacity of DBA recombiners.

Concentration thresholds that set the combustion mode

The hydrogen hazard is judged less by whether hydrogen is present than by how it would burn. The values below are representative for dry air at room temperature and atmospheric pressure; they shift with temperature, steam, turbulence and obstacles.

H₂ concentration (vol%)Combustion modeEffect on containment
Below about 4Flame propagation difficult (catalytic recombination still possible)None
4–9Propagation upward (about 4) → horizontal (about 6) → downward (about 9); burned fraction increasesSmall to moderate pressure rise
About 10 and aboveDeflagration in all directions; with obstacles and confinement, flame acceleration and deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT) possibleQuasi-static (AICC) to dynamic loads
18.3–59Classical detonation limits (experimental-condition values)Largest impulsive loads

The 10 % value is a regulatory design criterion, not a universal threshold for DDT. Even when the average is below 10 %, local accumulation and combustion loads are assessed separately.

Concentration alone is not enough. On the hydrogen–air–steam ternary diagram (Shapiro diagram), under representative conditions there is no flammable region when steam exceeds about 55 vol% (steam inerting, dependent on temperature and pressure). But if sprays or heat sinks condense the steam, the same amount of hydrogen can suddenly move into the flammable or detonable region (de-inerting). Limiting the oxygen concentration also suppresses combustion, which is the basis of the inerting strategy. Modern assessments go beyond a simple concentration and use the expansion-ratio σ criterion (flame acceleration) and the 7λ criterion (DDT).

Four control approaches

  1. Mixing: containment sprays, natural convection and fan coolers prevent local accumulation (the total amount is unchanged). Sprays, however, can condense steam and remove the steam-inert condition.
  2. Recombination (flameless removal): electric thermal recombiners (electrically heated to several hundred °C) or PARs (Pt/Pd catalyst, typically self-starting at about 1–2 vol%, depending on catalyst condition and humidity) convert H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O.
  3. Deliberate ignition: glow-plug igniters (surface about 900 °C) are energized early according to procedures, so the mixture burns off in small amounts at low concentration as soon as it becomes flammable. Igniting late, after hydrogen has accumulated, increases the loads.
  4. Inerting: nitrogen replacement before operation (BWR Mark I and II; under US rules O₂ below 4 vol%) or CO₂/N₂ injection after an accident rules out combustion altogether.

Dilution and venting involve releasing radioactive material, so they are judged together with depressurization means such as filtered venting.

Choice by containment type

  • Large dry PWR (most plants in South Korea): the large free volume keeps the average concentration low. Mixing plus recombination (PAR) is the basis, and APR1400 adds igniters.
  • BWR Mark I and II: the containment is small, so nitrogen inerting during operation is mandatory (50.44(b)(2)(i)).
  • Ice condenser and Mark III: the US requires igniter systems.

At Fukushima Daiichi (March 2011), hydrogen leaking from inerted Mark I containments accumulated in the upper part of the non-inerted reactor buildings and exploded in the buildings of Units 1, 3 and 4. The case shows that inerting protects only the inside of the containment vessel.

APR1400 hydrogen mitigation system (example)

  • Design basis: accommodate the hydrogen from a 100 % fuel-cladding metal–water reaction and keep the average concentration at or below 10 vol%
  • 30 PARs: 12 for design basis accidents (8 large units in the upper dome, 4 medium units at the IRWST and vent gates; safety class 3 in Korea and the UAE) + 18 for severe accidents (non-safety class), all seismic Category I. Unavailability of 25 % of the PARs is also assumed (failure to start in the Korean safety analysis; shutdown PRA in the US).
  • 10 igniters (8 in the US certified design): back up the PARs for rapid releases, placed near release sources
  • Hydrogen monitoring (Korea): two sensors measuring four points (two upper, two lower), indicating range 0–15 vol%
  • The earlier electric recombiners were replaced by PARs.

A rough sense of scale: if about 22.7 t of cladding in the active region (approximated as Zr) reacts completely, about 1.0 t of H₂ (about 498 kmol) is produced. Taking a free volume of about 89,000 m³, initial dry air at 1 atm and no removal, the mole fraction is about 11 % at 0 °C and about 12 % at 25 °C. This is an educational order-of-magnitude calculation showing why the 10 % criterion calls for hydrogen control equipment; it is not a calculation that sizes the equipment.

What has changed since the questions were set

  • The 2003 revision of 10 CFR 50.44 removed the US design basis LOCA hydrogen requirements (recombiners and purge) and shifted the focus to severe accident hydrogen. The "electric recombiner" in the 2010–2015 questions belongs to the design concept before this change.
  • In South Korea, PARs were installed in operating plants as a post-Fukushima action. After a 2021 whistleblower report, their performance was re-verified (reported to the NSSC at its 173rd and 194th meetings), and in August 2025 an operating license change to replace 18 PARs was approved.
  • Combustion or explosion of combustible gases must be evaluated as a threat in the accident management plan (the threat table is the current Appendix 3; it was Appendix 2 when the questions were set).

"At 4 % it explodes" is wrong. 4 % is the lower flammability limit for upward propagation; detonation needs much higher concentrations and particular geometric conditions. Turning igniters on late is actually more dangerous. Also remember that mixing evens out the concentration distribution but does not reduce the total amount.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition: a strategy of mixing, monitoring, removal and inerting that protects the containment and essential equipment from hydrogen deflagration and detonation in an accident.
  2. Sources: Zr–steam reaction (main), radiolysis, Al/Zn corrosion, MCCI (H₂, CO).
  3. Criteria: lower flammability limit for upward propagation about 4 vol% (condition dependent); classical detonation limits 18.3–59 %; local FA/DDT assessed separately; steam-inert when steam is high (representative 55 %).
  4. Design criterion: average below 10 vol% for uniform hydrogen from a 100 % cladding reaction (50.44(c), South Korean regulatory guideline); basis in Korean technical standard rule, Article 23(1)2.
  5. Recombination: electric (DBA, needs power); PAR (passive, finite removal rate).
  6. Igniters: early energization → continuous low-concentration burning; need power; late ignition increases loads.
  7. Inerting: nitrogen replacement in BWR Mark I and II (O₂ < 4 %); at Fukushima, hydrogen leaking into the buildings exploded.
  8. APR1400: 30 PARs (12 DBA + 18 severe accident), 10 igniters, hydrogen monitoring 0–15 % (per MDEP 2021).

This is a model answer, not an official solution. It reflects the criteria current as of 2026.

Exam relevance

Containment hydrogen control has appeared eight times in the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam: 73rd exam, session 4, Q5 (hydrogen sources in a LOCA); 81st exam, session 3, Q3 (hydrogen generation in containment); 2010 exam, session 3, Q2 (operating timing, advantages and disadvantages of igniters and PARs); 2011 exam, session 3, Q2 (hydrogen sources and removal equipment); 2015 exam, session 2, Q2 (comparison table of electric recombiners, igniters and PARs); 2019 exam, session 4, Q5 (APR1400 hydrogen sources and combustible-gas control); 2022 exam, session 2, Q2 (hydrogen generation mechanisms and why hydrogen control matters); and 2026 exam, session 3, Q4 (APR1400 severe accident mitigation features, hydrogen mitigation system). Typical questions ask for the hydrogen sources, a feature-by-feature comparison of the removal devices (actuation, timing, location, principle, pros and cons) and the APR1400 configuration. When a comparison table is requested, fill every row the question lists, and state that the 10 % figure is a uniform-average design criterion rather than the point at which hydrogen explodes.
