Radiation Protection & Emergency · C-RP-004

Radiological Emergency Planning Zones (PAZ, UPZ) — Public Protection Zones Set within Base Areas by Consultation and Approval

Why public protection is planned in two tiers, how the law's base-area range becomes an agreed boundary, and what the licensee must consider.

Concept C-RP-004
Definition

A radiological emergency planning zone is an area set under Article 20-2 of Korea's Act on Physical Protection and Radiological Emergency (the Radiological Emergency Act) because emergency measures need to be prepared intensively there to protect residents if a radiation emergency or radiological disaster occurs at a nuclear facility (Article 2(1)9 of the Act). It is divided into the precautionary action zone (PAZ) and the urgent protective action planning zone (UPZ).

A suitable first line for an answer: "An area where emergency measures are prepared intensively to protect residents in a nuclear accident, divided into the PAZ (3–5 km), centred on evacuation in advance, and the UPZ (20–30 km), centred on urgent protective actions based on assessment." That gives both the definition and the two-tier structure.

Principle and mechanism

Why two tiers

The means of reducing public dose in a severe accident are evacuation, sheltering, thyroid-blocking agents (stable iodine), relocation and food restrictions. Which of these works best depends on distance and time.

  • PAZ (close in): residents could receive high doses within a short time once a release begins. The aim is to evacuate as a precaution before the release, on the basis of the facility state alone (a red emergency, etc.), without waiting for measurements, to prevent severe deterministic effects.
  • UPZ (further out): the impact varies greatly with direction and weather. Sheltering and thyroid-blocking agents come first; the extent of evacuation and relief is then decided from radiological impact assessment and environmental monitoring results. If a major release is strongly threatened, however, evacuation can go ahead without waiting for measurements. The legal definition itself contains the words "based on the results of radiological impact assessment or environmental monitoring".

Distance criteria and the setting procedure

Article 20-2(1) of the Act has the NSSC publish the base area for each facility. For a power reactor, the PAZ radius is set within 3 km to 5 km and the UPZ radius within 20 km to 30 km of the installation point. In other words, what the law fixes is the range of the base area. The actual zone is fixed within this range by the following procedure:

  1. The NSSC publishes the base areas (they may be differentiated by facility characteristics such as thermal power, Enforcement Decree Article 22-2(1)).
  2. The licensee consults the provincial governor(s) concerned (Act, paragraph 2), considering local characteristics such as population distribution, road network and terrain, and the effectiveness of emergency measures.
  3. The licensee obtains NSSC approval (for setting, changing and removing alike, Act, paragraph 3).
  4. The licensee reflects the approved zone in its radiological emergency plan (Act, paragraph 4), and the local governments concerned prepare local radiological emergency plans (Decree Article 21).

The actual boundary is not an exact circle but an agreed boundary that takes account of administrative areas and terrain (in practice, whether a given town or district is included becomes the issue). So even for the same plant, the outer radius can differ in the direction of each local government.

What it means in a plant

Protective actions by zone and time

In a South Korean red emergency, the PAZ carries out precautionary evacuation before the release. In the UPZ, sheltering, evacuation and intake of thyroid-blocking agents are implemented according to dose assessment and environmental monitoring results and the decision of the off-site emergency management center. Table 4 of IAEA EPR-NPP (2013) recommends the following when a General Emergency is declared:

  • PAZ: immediate iodine thyroid blocking (ITB, without delaying evacuation) and safe evacuation to beyond the UPZ
  • UPZ: immediate sheltering and ITB; if a major release is possible, evacuation to beyond the UPZ without delaying the PAZ evacuation (step by step according to wind direction, etc.)
  • People who are difficult to evacuate (hospitals, care homes, etc.): ITB and sheltering in large buildings. "Safe evacuation" means evacuation that does not endanger the lives of those being evacuated.

After a release, measurements are compared with operational intervention levels (OILs), and actions are extended or reduced regardless of the zone boundaries. The default values are based on the dose rate 1 m above the ground.

What the licensee must consider when setting the zones (part (c) of the 2024 question)

Under Article 22-2(2) of the Enforcement Decree, the licensee submits the following to the provincial governor during consultation:

  • Population distribution out to the maximum radius of the base area: population of each sector cell divided into 16 directions from true north × 2 km bands (sum of village populations, split by area share where a village straddles cells) and population by administrative area
  • A detailed map showing administrative areas, the road network, and terrain such as mountains and rivers
  • Facility characteristics such as purpose of installation and thermal power

In practice, effectiveness factors are also reviewed: capacity and bottlenecks of evacuation routes, vulnerable facilities such as schools and hospitals, constraints on evacuation direction due to coastlines or mountains, the integrity of administrative boundaries (whether whole towns or districts are included), and the systems for warnings, shelters and distribution of protective drugs.

A South Korean case

The UPZ of the Kori plant in the direction of Busan was set at 20–21 km when the zones were reset in 2015. It was then widened to 28–30 km by NSSC approval on 28 December 2021, and the number of Busan districts included rose from 3 to 10. In the Ulsan direction the same plant's UPZ has been 30 km since 2015 — a case of different values being approved for different directions within the legal range.

Regulatory basis

  • Radiological Emergency Act (current Act No. 20720, in force 1 January 2026): Article 2(1)9 (definition), Article 20-2 (setting) [article newly inserted 21 May 2014], Article 20 (approval of the radiological emergency plan)
  • Amendment history: the PAZ and UPZ were introduced by Act No. 12665 (promulgated 21 May 2014). Under Addendum Article 1, item 9 and Article 20-2 took effect on 22 November 2014, six months after promulgation. Addendum Article 2 recognized the previous zones but required them to be reset within six months; site-by-site resetting and approval took place in May 2015. Before the amendment, power reactors had a single EPZ of 8–10 km radius.
  • Enforcement Decree (current Presidential Decree No. 36447): Article 22-2 (differentiated publication of base areas, consultation data), Article 22 (contents of the emergency plan), Article 35 (local government emergency exercises: comprehensive exercises at least once every 2 years, intensive exercises on sheltering, evacuation, distribution of protective drugs, etc. at least once a year)
  • IAEA: GSR Part 7 (2015) requires the PAZ and UPZ together with the extended planning distance (EPD) and the ingestion and commodities planning distance (ICPD). The suggested maximum radii in Table 3 of EPR-NPP (2013), for 1,000 MWt and above, are PAZ 3–5 km, UPZ 15–30 km, EPD 100 km and ICPD 300 km. The generic criteria are 100 mSv in the first 7 days (evacuation, sheltering), 50 mSv thyroid in the first 7 days (ITB) and 100 mSv per year (temporary relocation).
  • United States: 10 CFR 50.47(c)(2) sets a plume exposure EPZ of about 10 miles (16 km) and an ingestion EPZ of about 50 miles (80 km).
  • Lessons from Fukushima: before the accident Japan's EPZ was about 8–10 km. Right after the accident the evacuation order was widened step by step from 3 to 10 to 20 km, and a sheltering order was issued for 20–30 km; later some areas beyond 20 km were designated as deliberate evacuation areas. Japan changed to a PAZ of about 5 km and a UPZ of about 30 km in 2012, and the 2014 South Korean amendment follows the same trend. Health effects during the evacuation of vulnerable people were also reported, which is why the principle of "safe evacuation" is emphasized.

The size of a zone is not the limit radiation can reach but the extent of advance preparation. Actual actions can extend beyond the zones depending on the situation and the OILs.

Key points at a glance

10-point model answer: radiological emergency planning zones (PAZ, UPZ)

  1. Definition: an area where emergency measures are prepared intensively to protect residents in a radiation emergency or radiological disaster (Radiological Emergency Act Article 2(9)).
  2. Basis: Act Article 20-2. The NSSC publishes the base areas → the licensee consults the provincial governor → NSSC approval → reflected in the radiological emergency plan.
  3. PAZ: radius 3–5 km. Precautionary evacuation and thyroid-blocking agents on the facility state before release → prevent deterministic effects.
  4. UPZ: radius 20–30 km. Initial actions such as sheltering and drugs; evacuation and relief based on radiological impact assessment and environmental monitoring results (or a threatened major release).
  5. Considerations: population distribution (16 directions × 2 km), road network and terrain, facility characteristics such as thermal power, effectiveness of emergency measures.
  6. Boundary: agreed boundary considering administrative areas and terrain; the radius can differ by direction.
  7. Amendment: inserted 21 May 2014 (in force 22 November 2014), reset in May 2015; formerly a single EPZ of 8–10 km.
  8. IAEA: PAZ 3–5, UPZ 15–30, EPD 100, ICPD 300 km; after release, actions adjusted with OILs.
  9. Background: the Fukushima accident showed the limits of a single 10 km zone.
  10. Distinction: different from the EAB and LPZ (siting) and from the radiation controlled area (workers).

This is a model answer, not an official solution. It reflects the criteria current as of 2026.

Exam relevance

Emergency planning zones have appeared four times in the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam: 2009 exam, session 1, Q2 (radiological emergency planning zone versus radiation controlled area); 2011 exam, session 1, Q3 (radiological emergency planning zone); 2015 exam, session 1, Q2 (PAZ and UPZ); and 2024 exam, session 2, Q2, a 25-point descriptive question asking for (a) the definition, (b) the PAZ and UPZ and (c) what the licensee must consider when setting the zones. The 2009 and 2011 questions date from the single-EPZ era; a current answer should use the two-tier PAZ/UPZ structure and add one line on the former 8–10 km zone. Make clear that the law fixes the base-area range while the actual zone comes from consultation and NSSC approval, and that the radii are preparation ranges, not radiation limits.
