Radiation Protection & Emergency · C-RP-003

Emergency Action Levels (EAL) — The Decision Table That Sets the Emergency Class from Initiating Conditions and Thresholds

Observable thresholds in the licensee's plan that classify an accident within 15 minutes, from the three-barrier matrix to release and natural-hazard criteria.

Concept C-RP-003
Definition

Emergency action levels (EALs) are predetermined, observable thresholds and conditions used to classify the plant state into a radiation emergency class — instrument readings, radiation monitor indications, operating conditions, entry into emergency operating procedures and the like. The definitions of the classes are set by Article 17 of Korea's Act on Physical Protection and Radiological Emergency (the Radiological Emergency Act) and [Annex 3] of its Enforcement Decree. Plant-specific EALs are set in the licensee's radiological emergency plan (Chapter 4, "Emergency classification and declaration criteria") and approved by the NSSC (Article 20 of the Act).

A suitable first line for an answer: "Plant-specific, observable decision thresholds set in the licensee's radiological emergency plan so that accident conditions are classified into emergency classes quickly and consistently."

Principle and mechanism

Three layers: class ← initiating condition ← threshold

The EAL scheme has three layers:

  • Emergency class: white, blue and red in South Korea (UE, Alert, SAE and GE in the US)
  • Initiating condition (IC): the event or state that corresponds to a class (e.g. "two of three barriers lost + the remaining one potentially lost")
  • EAL: the specific indication that recognizes the IC (e.g. "core exit temperature (CET) of 649 °C or more"). Several EALs are attached to one IC with OR logic.

Operators look up the table to set the class. The US requires classification within 15 minutes of the indications becoming available (10 CFR 50 Appendix E IV.C.2), and South Korean plans also aim to declare within 15 minutes.

Recognition categories (NEI 99-01)

The US NEI 99-01 groups ICs by how they are recognized (recognition category). The categories in Rev.6 (November 2012):

Rev.6 also allows R in place of A, and licensee material based on Rev.7 (September 2024, endorsed by NRC RG 1.101 Rev.7 in September 2025) uses R.

The barrier matrix (Category F)

EALs judge the three directly monitorable barriers (fuel cladding, reactor coolant system RCS, containment). Each barrier has Loss and Potential Loss thresholds.

  • FA1 (Alert): loss or potential loss of either the cladding or the RCS
  • FS1 (SAE): loss or potential loss of any two barriers
  • FG1 (GE): loss of any two barriers + loss or potential loss of the third

A barrier is counted only once, since Loss includes Potential Loss. The cladding and RCS are weighted more heavily, so loss or potential loss of the containment alone is not FA1 and is judged by EALs in other categories.

The older South Korean plans used the same logic. In the Kori 1 plan (revision 23, 2014), red condition 2 is "two of three barriers lost + the remaining one at risk (2A, 2B, 2C)". Example indications (Kori 1):

  • Cladding: Loss at coolant I-131 dose equivalent of 300 µCi/cc or CET 649 °C (1,200 °F); Potential Loss at 1.0 µCi/cc or 371 °C (700 °F)
  • RCS: Loss when a LOCA or SGTR is in progress (emergency operating procedures Emergency-1 or Emergency-3); Potential Loss at unidentified leakage of 1 gpm or the pressurizer relief valve setpoint
  • Containment: Loss when the design limit pressure is exceeded, leak-tightness is lost or isolation fails; Potential Loss when the spray actuation setpoint is exceeded

What it means in a plant

Judging "a large release of radioactive material to the environment"

Red condition 1 of the older South Korean plans is an EAL that judges the release itself.

  • 1A: under actual weather conditions, 10 mSv/h (1 rem/h) whole body or 50 mSv/h (5 rem/h) thyroid at the site boundary or more. Judged from the public dose assessment program or from values calculated with radiation monitor readings and the release rate.
  • 1B: a release not seen by the effluent monitors. Judged by whether the projected dose rate calculated from containment activity, pressure and release rate, or the measured value at the site boundary, exceeds the same criteria.

The radiological category of NEI 99-01 steps by projected integrated dose, not dose rate. AA1, AS1 and AG1 are TEDE 0.1, 1 and 10 mSv (thyroid 0.5, 5 and 50 mSv), that is 1 %, 10 % and 100 % of the EPA PAG. There are three paths: monitor readings (sustained for 15 minutes), dose assessment with actual weather, and field surveys (dose rate of 1,000 mR/h expected to last 60 minutes or more). Even where the numbers look the same as the South Korean criteria (mSv/h), the dimension of the IC is different.

Events threatening core melt with a serious release (red 5A–5E)

Even before a barrier is lost, a combination of lost mitigating functions means red (Kori 1 example):

  • 5A: LOCA + no safety injection + threatened loss of containment integrity
  • 5B: loss of secondary heat removal + feed-and-bleed unavailable (condensate and feedwater failed, auxiliary feedwater unavailable for 30 minutes or more)
  • 5C: reactor fails to trip and stays critical + no safety injection + fuel damage
  • 5D: loss of all AC power + turbine-driven auxiliary feedwater pump unavailable (auxiliary feedwater unavailable for 30 minutes or more)
  • 5E: LOCA + loss of containment heat removal (rising recirculation sump temperature threatening cavitation of the low-pressure safety injection pumps)

The same event escalates over time: loss of AC power is white immediately, blue if it lasts 15 minutes or more, and red 5D when auxiliary feedwater is also unavailable for 30 minutes.

Earthquake and tsunami EALs and the organization by class

The natural-hazard EALs in the Kori 1 plan:

  • White: reference seismic monitor value at or above the operating basis earthquake (OBE, 0.10 g horizontal, 0.07 g vertical) and confirmed as a real earthquake; flooding of the component cooling seawater building or auxiliary building by flood or tsunami; 10-minute average wind speed of 33 m/s or more
  • Blue (hot shutdown or above): safe shutdown earthquake (SSE, 0.20 g and 0.14 g) or more; wind speed of 44 m/s or more
  • Red: when an earthquake at or above the SSE or another major accident seriously damages systems and a release is expected

If one unit declares an emergency because of an earthquake, the other units on the same site declare too; alarms from equipment faults or during testing are excluded. On the first declaration the technical support center and operational support center are activated. In blue and red, the emergency operations facility (the licensee's emergency headquarters) and the off-site emergency management center are activated.

Regulatory basis

  • Radiological Emergency Act, Article 17 (classes), Article 20 (approval of the radiological emergency plan); Enforcement Decree Article 19 and [Annex 3] (class definitions and response procedures, amended 25 October 2011).
  • NSSC Notice "Regulation on radiological emergency measures of nuclear licensees".
  • Licensee radiological emergency plan, Chapter 4: declaration conditions and criteria tables for each unit. Revision 21 (October 2013) "standardized the emergency action levels" and incorporated FSAR seismic values (Kori site example).
  • United States: 10 CFR 50.47(b)(4) (standard classification and EAL scheme), Appendix E IV.B (EALs based on in-plant instrument conditions; changes to the whole scheme require prior approval) and IV.C.2 (15-minute classification). NEI 99-01 Rev.6 (2012) was endorsed by the NRC in 2013, and Rev.7 (September 2024) by RG 1.101 Rev.7. Passive plants use NEI 07-01.
  • IAEA GSR Part 7 (2015): requires an emergency classification system based on predetermined criteria.
  • Changes since the questions were set: the 2009–2012 questions date from the era of plans with lists of declaration conditions. South Korean EALs used this format up to the 2014 editions, with only the barrier judgment following NEI logic. Whether they were later reorganized into a matrix could not be confirmed from public sources. The Ministry of Education, Science and Technology of the question era is now the Nuclear Safety and Security Commission.

The correspondence is approximate (white ≈ Alert, blue ≈ SAE, red ≈ GE), and assignments can differ: the old South Korean plans put a LOCA beyond charging pump capacity in blue, whereas in NEI an RCS loss alone is Alert. EOPs are procedures that mitigate an accident; EALs are criteria that classify it. EOP entry is sometimes used as an EAL indication.

Key points at a glance

10-point model answer: emergency action levels (EAL)

  1. Definition: observable thresholds (instruments, monitors, procedure entry) that classify the plant state into emergency classes.
  2. Basis: Radiological Emergency Act Article 17 and Decree Annex 3 (class definitions) → EALs in the licensee's radiological emergency plan, approved by the NSSC (Act Article 20).
  3. Structure: class ← initiating condition (IC) ← EALs (OR logic); classify and declare within 15 minutes.
  4. Recognition categories (NEI 99-01): radiation (A, R allowed), cold shutdown (C), ISFSI (E), barriers (F), hazards (H), systems (S).
  5. Barrier judgment: loss or potential loss of cladding, RCS, containment → one (cladding or RCS) Alert, two SAE, two lost + the third lost or potentially lost GE (≈ South Korean red).
  6. Release criterion (South Korean example): site boundary 10 mSv/h whole body or 50 mSv/h thyroid (assessment program, monitors, field measurements).
  7. Threatened core melt (red examples): LOCA + no SI; feedwater and F&B unavailable; ATWS + no SI; SBO + TDAFW unavailable; loss of containment heat removal.
  8. Natural hazards (example): above OBE → white; above SSE → blue or higher.
  9. Cautions: no declaration on an alarm alone (confirm it is real); highest class takes precedence; terminate after stabilizing below the white criteria.

This is a model answer, not an official solution. It reflects the criteria current as of 2026.

Exam relevance

Emergency action levels have appeared five times in the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam, all between 2009 and 2012, when the plans still listed declaration conditions class by class: 2009 exam, session 2, Q1 (class criteria with at least five declaration conditions per class); 2010 exam, session 2, Q2 (meaning of "a large release to the environment" and at least four red-emergency events threatening core melt with a serious release); 2011 exam, session 2, Q3 (examples of such events) and Q4 (how a large release is judged and the criteria); and 2012 exam, session 3, Q2 (declaration conditions for an earthquake or tsunami and the emergency organization at each stage). Answers should name the red 1A/1B paths and the 5A–5E events, explain the barrier logic, and point out that the South Korean release criterion is a dose rate while the NEI criterion is a projected dose.
