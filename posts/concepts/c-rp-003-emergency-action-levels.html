Radiation Protection & Emergency · C-RP-003

Emergency Action Levels (EAL) — The Decision Table That Sets the Emergency Class from Initiating Conditions and Thresholds

Observable thresholds in the licensee's plan that classify an accident within 15 minutes, from the three-barrier matrix to release and natural-hazard criteria.

Concept C-RP-003
Definition

Emergency action levels (EALs) are predetermined, observable thresholds and conditions used to classify the plant state into a radiation emergency class — instrument readings, radiation monitor indications, operating conditions, entry into emergency operating procedures and the like. The emergency classes themselves are defined in general terms (facility state and expected extent of impact); plant-specific EALs are set in the licensee's radiological emergency plan and approved by the regulator.

Put simply, EALs are plant-specific, observable decision thresholds set in the licensee's radiological emergency plan so that accident conditions are classified into emergency classes quickly and consistently.

Principle and mechanism

Three layers: class ← initiating condition ← threshold

The EAL scheme has three layers:

  • Emergency class: in the US, Unusual Event (UE), Alert, Site Area Emergency (SAE) and General Emergency (GE); other schemes use three classes
  • Initiating condition (IC): the event or state that corresponds to a class (e.g. "two of three barriers lost + the remaining one potentially lost")
  • EAL: the specific indication that recognizes the IC (e.g. "core exit temperature (CET) above a specified value"). Several EALs are attached to one IC with OR logic.

Operators look up the table to set the class. The US requires classification within 15 minutes of the indications becoming available (10 CFR 50 Appendix E IV.C.2) [4].

Recognition categories (NEI 99-01)

The US NEI 99-01 groups ICs by how they are recognized (recognition category). The categories in Rev.6 (November 2012) [2]:

The barrier matrix (Category F)

EALs judge the three directly monitorable barriers (fuel cladding, reactor coolant system RCS, containment). Each barrier has Loss and Potential Loss thresholds.

  • FA1 (Alert): loss or potential loss of either the cladding or the RCS
  • FS1 (SAE): loss or potential loss of any two barriers
  • FG1 (GE): loss of any two barriers + loss or potential loss of the third

A barrier is counted only once, since Loss includes Potential Loss. The cladding and RCS are weighted more heavily, so loss or potential loss of the containment alone is not FA1 and is judged by EALs in other categories.

Older list-format plans, modelled on the example initiating conditions in Appendix 1 of NUREG-0654, used the same logic: General Emergency example 2 there is "loss of 2 of 3 fission product barriers with a potential loss of 3rd barrier" [1]. Example PWR thresholds from NEI 99-01 Rev.6 (Table 9-F-3; actual values are site-specific) [2]:

  • Cladding: Loss at coolant dose-equivalent I-131 of 300 µCi/g, a containment radiation monitor reading above a set value, or a CET example of 1,200 °F (649 °C); Potential Loss at a CET example of 700 °F (371 °C)
  • RCS: Loss when RCS or SG tube leakage requires ECCS; Potential Loss when the leakage needs the standby charging pump or the PTS cooldown rate is exceeded
  • Containment: Loss when integrity is lost while isolation is required, an unisolable path exists or an SG is faulted outside containment; Potential Loss at containment pressure above a set value, an explosive mixture, or core cooling not restored within 15 minutes of entering the restoration procedure

What it means in a plant

Judging "a large release of radioactive material to the environment"

The list-format origin of this criterion is General Emergency example 1 in NUREG-0654 Appendix 1, which uses two paths [1]:

  • 1.a: effluent monitors detect levels corresponding to 1 rem/h (10 mSv/h) whole body or 5 rem/h (50 mSv/h) thyroid at the site boundary under actual meteorological conditions
  • 1.b: the same dose rates are projected from other plant parameters (containment radiation level with a leak rate appropriate for the containment pressure, with some confirmation from effluent monitors) or measured in the environs

The radiological category of NEI 99-01 steps by projected integrated dose, not dose rate. AA1, AS1 and AG1 are TEDE 0.1, 1 and 10 mSv (thyroid 0.5, 5 and 50 mSv), that is 1 %, 10 % and 100 % of the EPA PAG. There are three paths: monitor readings (sustained for 15 minutes), dose assessment with actual weather, and field surveys (dose rate of 1,000 mR/h expected to last 60 minutes or more). A dose-rate criterion (mSv/h) and a projected dose (mSv) have different dimensions, even if similar numbers appear [2].

Events threatening core melt with a serious release

Even before a barrier is lost, a combination of lost mitigating functions can call for the highest class. General Emergency example 4 in NUREG-0654 Appendix 1 ("plant conditions that make release of large amounts of radioactivity in a short time possible, e.g. any core melt situation") lists five example PWR sequences [1]:

  • Small or large LOCA with failure of ECCS → severe core degradation or melt within minutes to hours
  • Loss of the feedwater and condensate systems + failure of emergency feedwater for an extended period → core melt possible within hours
  • Transient requiring shutdown + failure to scram → core damage, or additional failure of core cooling and makeup
  • Loss of offsite and onsite power + total loss of emergency feedwater makeup for several hours → eventual core melt
  • Small LOCA with initially successful ECCS, then failure of containment heat removal over several hours → core melt and likely containment failure

The same event escalates over time. In NEI 99-01 Rev.6, loss of all AC power for more than 15 minutes (hot shutdown or above) is an SAE, a threshold assigned from PSA insights [2].

Natural-hazard EALs and the emergency facilities

Natural-hazard EALs are tied to the plant's design-basis values. NUREG-0654 Appendix 1 gives these examples [1]:

  • Alert: earthquake greater than operating basis earthquake (OBE) levels; flood, low water, tsunami, hurricane surge or seiche near design levels; any tornado striking the facility
  • SAE (plant not in cold shutdown): earthquake greater than safe shutdown earthquake (SSE) levels; flood or similar greater than design levels, or failure of protection of vital equipment at lower levels; sustained winds or tornadoes in excess of design levels
  • GE: major internal or external events substantially beyond the design basis that cause massive common damage to plant systems

OBE and SSE values are plant design values (for example, SSE 0.3 g for the APR1400 standard design [5]). In NEI 99-01 Rev.6 an earthquake exceeding the OBE is an Unusual Event (HU2) [2]. An alarm alone is not enough; the earthquake must be confirmed as real, and alarms from equipment faults or testing are excluded. Under NUREG-0654 the technical support center and operational support center are activated at Alert, with the emergency operations facility placed on standby [1].

Regulatory and design criteria

The references below are US and international examples.

  • United States: 10 CFR 50.47(b)(4) (standard classification and EAL scheme), Appendix E IV.B (EALs based on in-plant instrument conditions; changes to the whole scheme require prior approval) and IV.C.2 (15-minute classification) [4]. NEI 99-01 Rev.6 (2012) was endorsed by the NRC in 2013, and Rev.7 (September 2024) by RG 1.101 Rev.7 [3]. Passive plants use NEI 07-01.
  • IAEA GSR Part 7 (2015): requires an emergency classification system based on predetermined criteria [6].
  • Licensee radiological emergency plan: contains the declaration conditions and criteria tables for each unit.

Class assignments can differ between schemes: NUREG-0654 examples put primary coolant leakage above 50 gpm at Alert [1], whereas NEI judges RCS loss by whether safety injection is needed [2]. EOPs are procedures that mitigate an accident; EALs are criteria that classify it. EOP entry is sometimes used as an EAL indication.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition: observable thresholds (instruments, monitors, procedure entry) that classify the plant state into emergency classes.
  2. Basis: general class definitions → plant-specific EALs in the licensee's radiological emergency plan, approved by the regulator.
  3. Structure: class ← initiating condition (IC) ← EALs (OR logic); classify and declare within 15 minutes.
  4. Recognition categories (NEI 99-01): radiation (A, R allowed), cold shutdown (C), ISFSI (E), barriers (F), hazards (H), systems (S).
  5. Barrier judgment: loss or potential loss of cladding, RCS, containment → one (cladding or RCS) Alert, two SAE, two lost + the third lost or potentially lost GE.
  6. Release criterion: NEI uses projected dose (TEDE 0.1 / 1 / 10 mSv = 1 / 10 / 100 % of the PAG); NUREG-0654 examples used a site-boundary dose rate (1 rem/h whole body) — different dimensions.
  7. Threatened core melt (highest-class examples): LOCA + ECCS failure; loss of feedwater + extended loss of emergency feedwater; ATWS; SBO + loss of emergency feedwater; small LOCA followed by loss of containment heat removal.
  8. Natural hazards (NUREG-0654 examples): above OBE → Alert; above SSE → SAE or higher.
  9. Cautions: no declaration on an alarm alone (confirm it is real); highest class takes precedence; terminate once the declaring conditions are resolved and the plant is stable.
