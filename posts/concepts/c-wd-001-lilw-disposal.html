Waste, Fuel Cycle & Decommissioning · C-WD-001

Low- and Intermediate-Level Waste Disposal — Classification, Disposal Methods, Disposal Restrictions and the Gyeongju Facility

Permanent isolation without intent to retrieve: which waste class may go into which type of facility, behind which barriers, and for how long it must be controlled.

Concept C-WD-001
Definition

Disposal of low- and intermediate-level radioactive waste (LILW) is the permanent isolation of radioactive waste other than high-level waste in a disposal facility with no intention of retrieval. Engineered and natural barriers delay the time it takes radionuclides to reach the human environment, so that radioactivity decreases by decay in the meantime. It differs from storage, which assumes retrieval.

A good first line for an answer: "Waste is classified as intermediate, low or very low level according to its activity concentration (NSSC Notice), and is permanently isolated behind multiple barriers using the near-surface (cavern, surface or landfill-type) or deep disposal method allowed for each class."

Principle and mechanism

Classification — 100 times the clearance level and Table 2 are the boundaries

Article 2(1) of the Enforcement Decree of Korea's Nuclear Safety Act defines waste whose activity concentration and heat generation rate are at or above values set by the NSSC as high-level waste, and the rest as LILW. The high-level threshold is a heat generation rate of 2 kW/m³ or more and a concentration of alpha-emitting nuclides with half-lives of 20 years or more of 4,000 Bq/g or more (both conditions). The subdivision within LILW is set by Article 3 of the NSSC Notice "Regulation on Radioactive Waste Classification and Clearance Criteria".

Disposal methods and disposal restrictions — Articles 4 and 5 of the Notice

Article 4 of the same Notice divides disposal methods into deep disposal and near-surface (shallow-land) disposal. Near-surface disposal is further divided into three types.

  • Cavern disposal: disposal in an underground cavern or within rock, with natural barriers or engineered barriers
  • Surface disposal: disposal near the ground surface with natural barriers and engineered barriers
  • Landfill-type disposal: disposal by burial near the ground surface with natural barriers

Surface and landfill-type disposal share the same location; the difference is whether engineered barriers are present. Article 5 (disposal restrictions) sets the methods each class may not use. Very low level waste may use any method. Low level waste may not use landfill-type disposal, and intermediate level waste may not use surface or landfill-type disposal. High-level waste may not use near-surface disposal. The NSSC may, however, make exceptions when it recognizes them as necessary for efficient disposal.

Multiple barriers and time — why 300 years

The engineered barriers run in this order: waste form → drum → concrete vault or silo → backfill → cover (drainage and sealing layers). Around them is the natural barrier of rock, soil and the groundwater environment. The barriers provide isolation, containment and retardation of radionuclide release, and time for decay. The main radionuclides in LILW are Co-60 (half-life 5.27 years) and Cs-137 (30.1 years). In 300 years Cs-137 passes about 10 half-lives and falls to about 1/1,000. Accordingly, Article 99(2) of the Enforcement Decree sets the post-closure control period at 200 years or less for cavern disposal and 300 years or less for other near-surface disposal. Where a site has several facilities, the longest period applies to all of them.

What it means in a plant — the Gyeongju facility

South Korea's only disposal facility is the Gyeongju LILW disposal facility operated by the Korea Radioactive Waste Agency (KORAD). The site in Munmudaewang-myeon (formerly Yangbuk-myeon), Gyeongju, was confirmed by a local referendum in November 2005.

  • Phase 1, cavern disposal: six rock silos 80–130 m below sea level, 100,000 drums (200 L). It passed the NSSC pre-operational inspection in December 2014 and began disposal in July 2015. It can accept waste up to intermediate level.
  • Phase 2, surface disposal: 125,000 drums of low level and below. It consists of 20 concrete vaults (20 m × 20 m × 10 m high); when a vault is full, it is sealed with a top slab and cover. Construction started in 2022; it passed the NSSC pre-operational inspection in March 2026 and, after completion in May, full operation is planned for the second half of the year. Phases 1 and 2 together hold 225,000 drums.
  • Phase 3, landfill-type: planned for very low level waste and below.
  • Control period: KORAD has set a post-closure control period of 300 years for the phase 1 cavern facility as well.

Waste shipped from plants must meet the NSSC Notice "Regulation on the Delivery of LILW" (currently No. 2024-13, 2024.12.31) and KORAD's acceptance criteria. The main requirements are:

  • Solid, stable, non-dispersible form, with free water at 0.5 % of volume or less (1 % for high-integrity containers)
  • Nuclides accounting for at least 95 % of the total activity identified, and concentrations of H-3, C-14, Co-60, Cs-137 and other nuclides determined
  • No removable surface contamination
  • Explosive, flammable, corrosive and infectious materials removed, and chelating agents limited

Harm-prevention criteria and performance objectives

Article 64(1)3 of the Nuclear Safety Act (licensing criteria for disposal facilities) requires that there be no hindrance to preventing harm from radioactive materials. Article 174 of the Enforcement Decree sets two criteria: concentrations of discharged liquid and gaseous radioactive materials must meet the Commission's standards, and any other radiation-harm-prevention standards set by the Commission must be met. The performance objectives of the disposal-facility safety assessment are:

  • Post-closure normal scenario: individual annual dose of 0.1 mSv (Article 6 of NSSC Notice No. 2017-62, "Radiation Harm Prevention Criteria for LILW Disposal Facilities")
  • Probabilistic events: annual risk of 10⁻⁶ or less (Article 6 of the same Notice)
  • Human intrusion: 1 mSv/year or less after the control period, ALARA
  • Accidents during operation: 5 mSv

Ending institutional control is also possible only if these performance objectives are met ("Technical Standards for the Operation, Closure and Post-Closure Control of LILW Disposal Facilities").

Siting criteria and social factors

The NSSC Notice "Technical Standards for the Location of LILW Disposal Facilities" examines meteorology, ground surface, geology, surface water, groundwater, earthquakes, ecology and water resources. The key criteria are:

  • Geology: homogeneous bedrock that retards radionuclide migration. Areas prone to erosion, landslides or volcanism are unsuitable; for cavern disposal, limestone and heavily fractured rock are unsuitable
  • Surface water and groundwater: above the predicted flood level, with low groundwater velocity and long delay times
  • Earthquakes: active fault zones and their vicinity are unsuitable
  • Water resources and other: water-source protection zones, and land crossed by airfields, high-voltage lines or gas pipelines, are unsuitable

Where requirements fall short, engineered barriers compensate. Social factors include local acceptance (referendum), support for the host region (special act), information disclosure and transparency, and transport routes. The confirmation of Gyeongju after the failures at Anmyeon-do, Gureop-do and Buan is the classic example.

Regulatory basis

  • Korea's Nuclear Safety Act: Article 2 (definitions), Articles 63–64 (construction and operating licenses for disposal facilities and licensing criteria), Article 70 (disposal restrictions)
  • Enforcement Decree: Article 2(1) (delegation of classification, amended 2014.9.11), Article 99(2) (post-closure control period, added 2016.12.22), Article 174 (harm prevention)
  • NSSC Notices: the classification regulation (No. 2014-003 → No. 2020-6 → No. 2023-7 → currently No. 2025-11, in force 2025.12.31, uranium added to Table 1), the delivery regulation, the location technical standards, and the technical standards for operation, closure and post-closure control (No. 2024-13)
  • IAEA: GSG-1 (2009; six classes EW, VSLW, VLLW, LLW, ILW, HLW), SSR-5 (2011; passive safety, multiple safety functions, institutional control)
  • Changes since the questions were set: the 2001 and 2004 questions date from a time when no site had been secured. The 2011 question fell under the Ministry of Education, Science and Technology notice system. The three-way classification and the disposal restrictions were introduced in 2014, the control period in 2016, and operation of the surface facility was added in 2026.

Disposal has no intention of retrieval; storage assumes retrieval. Clearance (self-disposal) releases material from regulatory control so that it becomes ordinary waste, which is different from disposal in a disposal facility.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition: waste other than high-level waste permanently isolated behind multiple barriers with no intention of retrieval (distinct from storage).
  2. Classification (Notice Art. 3): very low (clearance level to 100×) / low (100× to Table 2) / intermediate (Table 2 and above).
  3. Disposal methods (Art. 4): deep / near-surface = cavern, surface (natural + engineered), landfill-type (natural only).
  4. Disposal restrictions (Art. 5): no landfill-type for low level, no surface or landfill-type for intermediate level, no near-surface disposal for high level.
  5. Multiple barriers: waste form → drum → vault or silo → backfill → cover + natural barrier.
  6. Safety functions: isolation, retardation, decay (Cs-137 over 300 years ≈ 1/1,000).
  7. Performance objectives: 0.1 mSv/year in the normal scenario, 1 mSv/year for human intrusion.
  8. Control period (Decree Art. 99): cavern 200 years or less, near-surface 300 years or less.
  9. Gyeongju: phase 1 cavern, 100,000 drums (operating since 2015); phase 2 surface, 125,000 drums (completed 2026); phase 3 landfill-type planned.
  10. Acceptance criteria: free water 0.5 % or less, 95 % of nuclides identified, hazardous materials removed.

Exam relevance

In the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam LILW disposal appeared six times between 2001 and 2026: 63rd exam (2001), session 2, Q3 (siting conditions for a radioactive waste disposal site); 73rd exam (2004), session 3, Q5 (five overseas disposal facilities); 2011 exam, session 3, Q1 (advantages and disadvantages of each LILW disposal method and selection factors); 2015 exam, session 2, Q4 (waste classification, disposal methods, disposal restrictions and location criteria); 2020 exam, session 1, Q10 (harm-prevention criteria for LILW disposal facilities); and 2026 exam, session 2, Q6 (definitions such as clearance, very low level waste and surface disposal). Questions range from short definitions to descriptive answers on classification, methods and siting. Quote the Article 4 wording for the definitions of the disposal methods, and keep surface (natural and engineered barriers) and landfill-type (natural barriers only) distinct.
