Waste, Fuel Cycle & Decommissioning · C-WD-003

Radioactive Waste Classification — High-Level Thresholds, Intermediate/Low/Very-Low Boundaries and Comparison with the IAEA and US Systems

High level is tested first on two conditions; everything else is placed on a nuclide-specific concentration ladder that decides where the waste may be disposed of.

Concept C-WD-003
Definition

Radioactive waste classification sorts radioactive waste into classes according to activity concentration, heat generation rate and half-life. Because each class has its own permitted disposal methods and level of control, classification is the starting point for ensuring disposal safety.

A good first line for an answer: "Under Article 2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Nuclear Safety Act, waste is divided into high-level and low- and intermediate-level waste; LILW is further divided by activity concentration into intermediate, low and very low level under an NSSC Notice, and waste below the clearance level is released from regulatory control (clearance)."

Principle and mechanism

Legal structure — Act → Enforcement Decree → two Notices

Article 2, item 18 of Korea's Nuclear Safety Act defines radioactive waste as "radioactive material, or material contaminated by it, that is to be disposed of". The parenthetical includes spent fuel that has been decided to be disposed of under Article 36-2 (before the amendment of 2025.10.1 the reference was to Article 35(4)). Article 2(1) of the Enforcement Decree defines waste whose activity concentration and heat generation rate are at or above values set by the NSSC as high-level waste, and the rest as LILW. It also delegates the subdivision within LILW to the NSSC, which sets it by concentration.

The classification notice is the "Regulation on Radioactive Waste Classification and Clearance Criteria". The current version is NSSC Notice No. 2025-11 (in force 2025.12.31).

The boundaries are a ladder of nuclide-specific concentrations

The boundaries within LILW are not single values. The two lower boundaries are the Table 1 clearance level and 100 times it, and the low/intermediate boundary is the Table 2 nuclide concentration. For nuclides not in Table 2, the disposal limit concentration in the disposal operator's acceptance criteria is used.

(Units: Bq/g.) The Table 2 values are conversions from old Ci units. For example, Cs-137 1.11×10⁶ Bq/g is 30 μCi/g, and Co-60 3.70×10⁷ Bq/g is 1 mCi/g. When several nuclides are mixed, the sum Σ(Cᵢ/CL,i) is used for the decision. Deliberate mixing or dilution to meet the clearance level or clearance dose is prohibited (Notice Article 8(2)).

Definitions related to clearance — preparing for the 2026 exam, session 2, Q6

Article 2 of the same Notice defines the following terms.

  • Clearance (self-disposal): managing waste whose nuclide concentrations are confirmed to be below the clearance level as non-radioactive waste — by incineration, landfill, recycling and so on — by excluding it from the scope of the Nuclear Safety Act
  • Clearance dose criterion: the value at which the expected individual annual dose from clearance is below 10 μSv and the collective annual dose is below 1 man·Sv
  • Detection target value: a target activity concentration for detection set by the clearance operator itself
  • Minimum detectable concentration (MDC): the lowest detectable concentration, determined by measurement conditions such as the instrument, sample size, recovery and counting time. The detection target value is set sufficiently below the clearance level, and the actual MDC must be at or below the detection target value (Article 10(3)).

Surface disposal is defined in Article 4 as "disposal near the ground surface with natural barriers and engineered barriers".

What it means in a plant

  • Determining the disposal route: the class determines the disposal method. Gyeongju phase 1 (cavern) accepts waste up to intermediate level, phase 2 (surface) low level and below, and phase 3 (landfill-type) very low level waste.
  • Decommissioning waste: most of the concrete and metal from decommissioning a plant is very low level or subject to clearance. The very low level class and landfill-type disposal are key means of reducing decommissioning waste volumes.
  • Operational waste: concentrated liquid waste, spent resin and dry active waste are mostly low level; some spent resin and filters become intermediate level.
  • Nuclide identification: on delivery to the disposal facility, nuclides accounting for at least 95 % of the total activity must be identified and the concentrations of H-3, C-14, Co-60, Cs-137 and others determined. Difficult-to-measure nuclides (DTM) are estimated with scaling factors.
  • Spent fuel: before a decision to dispose of it, it is not radioactive waste but material in storage.

Regulatory basis

  • Korea's Nuclear Safety Act Article 2 (definitions), Article 70 (disposal restrictions) / Enforcement Decree Article 2(1) (delegation of the LILW subdivision by the amendment of 2014.9.11), Article 107 (clearance) / Enforcement Rule Article 94
  • Classification Notice: No. 2014-003 (2014.9.16; three-way classification, disposal methods and disposal restrictions introduced) → No. 2020-6 → No. 2023-7 → No. 2025-11 (U-234, U-235 and U-238 at 1 Bq/g added to Table 1)
  • IAEA GSG-1 (2009): replaced the 1994 three-class scheme (exempt / low and intermediate level, short- and long-lived / high level) with six classes. Numerical boundaries such as the 1994 HLW thermal power of 2–20 kW/m³ are not specified in GSG-1.
  • US 10 CFR 61.55: divides LLW for near-surface disposal into Classes A, B and C; waste above Class C (GTCC) is generally not suitable for near-surface disposal. GTCC is not high-level waste.
  • Changes since the questions were set: the 2008 question dates from the two-class period (high level / LILW). The three-way intermediate/low/very-low classification was introduced in 2014.9, and Table 1 was amended in 2025.12.

The correspondence between classes is conceptual. South Korea sets boundaries by Table 2 concentrations, the IAEA by the need for isolation (long-lived nuclides, heat), and the US by nuclide tables based on intruder dose. The IAEA treats half-lives under about 30 years as short-lived, and VSLW covers nuclides with half-lives of about 100 days or less.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition (Act Article 2): radioactive material and the like that is to be disposed of, including spent fuel decided for disposal.
  2. High level (Decree Article 2, Notice): α nuclides (T½ ≥ 20 years) at 4,000 Bq/g or more and heat generation rate of 2 kW/m³ or more.
  3. LILW: everything other than high level → three classes under Article 3 of the NSSC Notice.
  4. Intermediate level: at or above the Table 2 nuclide concentrations.
  5. Low level: 100 times the clearance level or more, below Table 2.
  6. Very low level: at or above the clearance level, below 100 times it.
  7. Clearance: below the clearance level, or meeting the clearance dose (individual below 10 μSv/year, collective below 1 man·Sv/year) → released from regulation.
  8. Example (Cs-137): boundaries at 0.1 / 10 / 1.11×10⁶ Bq/g.
  9. Disposal restrictions (Article 5): no landfill-type for low level, no surface or landfill-type for intermediate level, no near-surface disposal for high level.
  10. Comparison: IAEA GSG-1 six classes (EW, VSLW, VLLW, LLW, ILW, HLW); US Classes A, B, C and GTCC.

Exam relevance

In the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam waste classification appeared five times between 2008 and 2026: 2008 exam, session 1, Q6 (South Korean classification criteria for high-level waste); 2015 exam, session 2, Q4 (waste classification, disposal methods, disposal restrictions and location criteria); 2016 exam, session 1, Q10 (definition and classification of radioactive waste); 2024 exam, session 4, Q2 (South Korean waste classification and criteria, with spent fuel storage); and 2026 exam, session 2, Q6 (definitions of clearance, clearance dose, very low level waste, MDC and surface disposal). Most are short definition or classification items. State that the high-level criterion needs both conditions ("and"), that 4,000 Bq/g is not the low/intermediate boundary (that boundary is nuclide-specific, Table 2), and that very low level waste is still radioactive waste while clearance applies only below the clearance level.
