Reactor Coolant Selection and Reactor-Type Comparison — How Water, Gas, Liquid Metal and Molten Salt Set Pressure and Temperature

Why water reactors need 15.5 MPa while sodium, lead and salt run hot near atmospheric pressure: heat capacity, conductivity, pumping power, activation and chemistry compared.

Concept C-PE-001
Definition

A reactor coolant is the fluid that removes the heat generated in the core and carries it toward the steam generators and turbine. A good coolant stores and conducts plenty of heat (high ρcₚ and k), flows with little pumping power, does not boil at low pressure, absorbs few neutrons and is little activated, is chemically compatible with structural materials, and is cheap.

The choice of coolant largely fixes a reactor type's pressure, temperature and neutron spectrum.

The grouping follows the common headings of coolant textbooks [1][2]. No coolant meets every requirement, so what was sacrificed and which design features compensate for it define the character of each reactor type.

The requirements in numbers

To carry thermal power Q with a temperature rise ΔT requires a mass flow ṁ = Q/(cₚΔT). Assuming a turbulent friction factor f ∝ Re−0.2, the same flow geometry and constant properties, the pumping power is Ẇ ∝ μ0.2/(ρ²cₚ2.8) (the same form as El-Wakil's coolant figure of merit) [2]. It is a relative hydraulic power under identical conditions, not an actual pump or blower power ratio.

Properties were calculated from IAPWS-IF97 (water) [3], Fink & Leibowitz (Na) [4], the OECD/NEA handbook (Pb) [5], Romatoski & Hu (FLiBe) [6] and the ideal-gas law (gases).

Water and molten salt are strong at storing heat (ρcₚ); liquid metals are overwhelmingly strong at conducting it (k). FLiBe has a higher ρcₚ than water but a high viscosity, so its index is 3.5. Gases have a ρcₚ below 1/100 of water's, so they are run at high pressure to raise density and with a large ΔT. Because Ẇ ∝ ΔT−2.8, changing only ΔT brings an SFR (about 150 °C) down to 0.85, about the level of water (about 33 °C), and an HTGR (about 500 °C) down to about 15 (first-order approximation).

Nuclear properties and activation

Thermal-neutron absorption cross sections are H 0.332 b, D 0.00052 b, Na 0.53 b, Pb 0.17 b, and almost zero for He-4 [7]. Fast reactors must avoid moderation, so they use Na, Pb or He rather than hydrogen. Each coolant produces different activation products.

  • H₂O: O-16(n,p)N-16 (T½ 7.13 s, γ 6.13 MeV). It disappears within minutes of shutdown.
  • D₂O: D(n,γ)T (T½ 12.3 years). Tritium has to be managed.
  • Na: Na-24 (T½ 15.0 h, γ 1.37 and 2.75 MeV). This is why an intermediate sodium loop is used.
  • Pb-Bi: Bi-209(n,γ) → Po-210 (T½ 138 d, α).
  • FLiBe: tritium is produced from Li-6 and Li-7, so highly enriched Li-7 is needed.

The saturation pressure of light water is 7.0 MPa at 286 °C, 10.0 MPa at 311 °C and 15.5 MPa at 344.8 °C (IAPWS-IF97) [3]. At 15.5 MPa with a hot-leg temperature of 323.9 °C, a PWR keeps about 21 °C of bulk subcooling to prevent bulk boiling. This is a separate quantity from the DNBR margin. As a result, the outlet temperature of water-cooled reactors is tied to around 330 °C, and their thermal efficiency stays at about 33–35 %. Liquid metals and molten salts have high boiling points and reach 500 °C or more near atmospheric pressure. They have no depressurization boiling after a break, but freezing and chemical reactions become the problems to solve.

CO₂ (AGR) is cheap and runs hot, but graphite oxidation has to be managed. Pb does not react violently with water, but it is heavy and corrodes steel, so its oxygen concentration is controlled.

Light-water and heavy-water reactors

In a light-water reactor, H₂O serves as both coolant and moderator, and reactivity is controlled with boric acid. A heavy-water reactor (CANDU-6) separates the hot, high-pressure D₂O coolant (380 pressure tubes) from the cold, low-pressure D₂O moderator (calandria) [9]. Absorption in D is about 1/640 of that in H, so natural uranium can be used. The price is the cost of D₂O, tritium, and a small positive coolant void coefficient. The fundamental differences between CANDU and the PWR are the moderator, the fuel (natural vs enriched), pressure tubes vs a pressure vessel, on-power vs shutdown refueling, and the reactivity control and shutdown systems (SDS1 and SDS2).

The six Gen-IV systems

The six systems selected in the GIF technology roadmap (2002, updated 2014) are distinguished by their coolants [11]: VHTR (He, 900–1,000 °C), GFR (He, 850 °C), SFR (Na, 500–550 °C), LFR (Pb, 480–570 °C), MSR (fluoride salt, 700–800 °C) and SCWR (supercritical water, 510–625 °C). Outlet temperatures follow the GIF comparison table and differ between sources. The common direction is to leave the light-water saturation curve for high temperature at low pressure (or supercritical conditions).

Regulatory and design criteria

Examples of internationally used design criteria:

  • 10 CFR 50 Appendix A (United States): GDC 10 (fuel design limits), GDC 11 (reactor inherent protection), GDC 14 (reactor coolant pressure boundary), GDC 33, 34 and 35 (coolant makeup, residual heat removal, emergency core cooling). They were written with light-water reactors in mind [12].
  • NRC RG 1.232 Rev. 0 (April 2018): principal design criteria for non-light-water reactors, giving ARDC adapted from the GDC plus SFR-DC and MHTGR-DC [13]. For example, GDC 14, which assumes a high-pressure boundary, does not fit reactor types that operate near atmospheric pressure as written.
  • IAEA SSR-2/1 Rev. 1 (2016): contains design requirements for the reactor coolant system (Requirement 47 and others) [14]. It is mainly a water-cooled reactor standard and is applied in a graded manner to other types.
  • Recent developments: the Gen-IV roadmap update (2014) and the non-LWR design criteria (RG 1.232, 2018) changed the regulatory framing; coolant properties and the selection principles are unchanged.

"Sodium stores heat better than water" is a wrong statement. Its thermal conductivity is about 120 times higher, but its volumetric heat capacity is about 1/4.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition: a fluid that removes and transports core heat (in LWRs it is also the moderator).
  2. Thermal: high ρcₚ → less flow; high k → small cladding temperature difference.
  3. Hydraulic: low viscosity → low pumping power, Ẇ ∝ μ0.2/(ρ²cₚ2.8).
  4. Phase change: high boiling point → low-pressure operation (water needs 15.5 MPa); low melting point → no freezing.
  5. Nuclear: low σa; for fast reactors, little moderation (no hydrogen).
  6. Activation: low induced activity, short half-lives (N-16 7 s, Na-24 15 h, Po-210).
  7. Chemical: compatible with structures, no reaction with water or air, stable under radiolysis.
  8. Economy and safety: cheap, negative void coefficient, no fire or explosion.
  9. Examples: H₂O (PWR, BWR), D₂O (PHWR), CO₂ (AGR), He (HTGR, VHTR, GFR), Na (SFR), Pb (LFR), fluoride salt (MSR).
