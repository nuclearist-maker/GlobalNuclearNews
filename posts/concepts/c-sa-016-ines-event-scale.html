International Nuclear and Radiological Event Scale (INES) — Levels 0–7, Rated in Three Areas, Highest Value Wins

A logarithmic 0–7 communication scale: each event is rated in three areas and the highest level is taken.

Concept C-SA-016
Definition

The International Nuclear and Radiological Event Scale (INES) is a 0–7 scale for communicating the safety significance of events involving radiation sources to the public quickly and consistently. It was developed in 1990 by the IAEA and the OECD/NEA, and the current reference document is the INES User's Manual, 2008 Edition (published 2009; emended version March 2013).

A good first line for an exam answer: "A scale for international communication that assesses an event in three areas — people and environment, radiological barriers and control, and defence in depth — and assigns the highest of the three levels; levels 1–3 are incidents and levels 4–7 are accidents." That one sentence carries both the definition and the structure.

Principle and mechanism

Levels 1–3 are incidents and levels 4–7 are accidents. The design intent is a logarithmic scale on which severity rises about tenfold with each level. Industrial-safety accidents such as electric shock or falls, security events and medical exposures of patients are outside its scope. (Korean-language names for the levels vary between sources; the English names above are the Manual's.)

Three assessment areas and the highest-value rule

  1. People and environment: actual exposures and releases. Levels 4–7 are judged by the amount released; below that, by individual dose and the number of people exposed. A public effective dose above 10 mSv or a worker exceeding the annual dose limit is at least level 2; more than 10 times the worker annual limit, or a non-lethal deterministic effect (including a likely one), is at least level 3; a radiation death is at least level 4.
  2. Radiological barriers and control: applies only to large facilities whose site boundary is fixed in the licence. Release from the fuel assemblies of more than about 0.1 % of the core inventory is level 4; melting of, or release from the assemblies of, a few per cent or more of the core is level 5. A gamma + neutron dose rate (at 1 m from the source) above 50 mSv/h in an operating area that needs no special access permit is level 2; above 1 Sv/h it is level 3.
  3. Defence in depth: events with no actual consequences in which safety provisions did not perform as expected. Defence-in-depth criteria alone give at most level 3, and additional factors such as common-cause failure, procedural problems or safety culture can raise the level by one where warranted (cap 3, no double counting).

The final level is the highest of the levels obtained in the three areas. Level 1 covers only defence-in-depth degradation, and levels 4–7 can be reached only with actual consequences.

Release criteria — I-131 equivalent

For atmospheric releases from nuclear facilities, levels 4–7 are judged not by dose but by the release converted to I-131 equivalent, in TBq (the death and exposure criteria also apply separately). The reason is that in a large release, successful protective actions such as evacuation keep the actual dose from growing in proportion to the release. The Manual (Section 2.2) defines:

  • Level 4: of the order of tens to hundreds of TBq (protective actions other than local food controls generally not needed)
  • Level 5: of the order of hundreds to thousands of TBq
  • Level 6: of the order of thousands to tens of thousands of TBq
  • Level 7: more than several tens of thousands of TBq

Early estimates are necessarily approximate, so the definitions avoid precise values. Instead, a footnote recommends interpreting the boundaries as roughly 500, 5,000 and 50,000 TBq. The conversion multiplies each nuclide's release by the factor in Manual Table 2 and sums the results. Representative factors: Cs-137 40, Sr-90 20, Co-60 50, Cs-134 17 (2013 emended version; 3 in the 2009 printing), H-3 0.02, Pu-239 10,000. Noble gases are effectively set to zero in this conversion. Transport and radiation-source events are judged by multiples of the D2 value.

Defence in depth — initiating-event frequency categories

For reactors at power (including hot shutdown and start-up), the defence-in-depth assessment sets a base level from the combination of initiating-event frequency and operability of the safety functions (Manual Section 5). The frequency used is the one assumed in that plant's safety case.

CategoryDefinitionFrequency
ExpectedExpected to occur one or more times during the plant lifetime> 10⁻²/yr
PossibleNot expected, but probability of occurrence during the lifetime greater than about 1 %10⁻⁴ – 10⁻²/yr
UnlikelyConsidered in the design but rarer≤ 10⁻⁴/yr
Beyond designVery low frequency, not normally included in the safety analysis—

If an initiating event actually occurred, Table 9 is used. Even with full safety functions the result is 0 for expected, 1 for possible and 2 for unlikely initiators (the initiator was not prevented). If only a degradation of function was found, with no initiating event, Table 10 is used: if the operational limits and conditions (OL&C) of the technical specifications are met the level is 0, and if the function is inadequate it is 3 for expected, 2 for possible and 1 for unlikely initiators. For example, discovering during power operation that all emergency diesel generators had been unavailable for a month could be level 3 if no alternative equipment was available.

What it means in a plant

  • Rating procedure in South Korea: the basis is the NSSC Notice "Regulation on Reporting and Disclosure of Accidents and Failures at Nuclear Facilities" (current No. 2025-4). The licensee makes an oral report within the time limit in the annex (for power reactors, to the head of the regional office), and by the next working day enters a provisional level, assessed under the rating guideline in Appendix 2, in the initial written report (with an INES rating form added for level 2 or above). The NSSC's Event Rating Committee decides the final level each quarter (or as needed) and publishes it.
  • Public and international disclosure: the licensee discloses any orally reported event on its website and to the press within 24 hours of occurrence. The NSSC notifies the IAEA through the INES information system (NEWS) of events provisionally rated level 2 or above, among others. The Manual also recommends notification of level 2 and above within 24 hours where possible.
  • Definitions of accident and failure in the Notice: Article 3 defines an "accident" as level 4 or above and a "failure" as level 3 or below, matching the INES accident (4–7) / incident (1–3) split.
  • Events during start-up: an event during start-up, such as the Shin-Kori Unit 1 containment spray event (17 September 2009), is assessed under Section 5 of the Manual, the same as power operation. The assessment looks in turn at whether an initiating event actually occurred, the operability of the affected safety functions, and additional factors (procedures, safety culture).
  • Relation to event reporting: the reporting deadlines and whether an event is subject to rating are listed by event type in the annex of the Notice.

Regulatory basis

  • IAEA·OECD/NEA, INES User's Manual 2008 Edition (2009; emended March 2013): supersedes the 2001 edition and others. The scale began in 1990 for nuclear facilities, was extended in 1992 to radioactive material and transport events, and the 2008 edition integrated the guidance for radiation sources and transport.
  • NSSC Notice "Regulation on Reporting and Disclosure of Accidents and Failures at Nuclear Facilities": Article 3 (definitions), Article 5 (oral, initial written and detailed reports), Article 7 (provisional level), Article 8 (final level by the rating committee), Article 10 (24-hour disclosure), Article 11 (notification to the IAEA). The 2025 revision (No. 2025-4) added a 4-hour report for exposures requiring transfer to a medical institution and immediate reporting of acute effects. "Immediately" means within 30 minutes.
  • Korea's Nuclear Safety Act, Article 92: when a failure or similar event occurs, the licensee reports to the NSSC without delay after taking safety measures.
  • Changes since the questions were set: the 76th exam question dates from the 2001-edition era. The 2001 edition's "off-site / on-site impact" was reorganized in the 2008 edition into "people and environment" and "radiological barriers and control" (worker dose moved to people and environment), but South Korean report forms still use "on-site / off-site impact". Since October 2011 the regulator has been the NSSC.

The Manual states explicitly that INES does not replace national emergency response criteria and that it is inappropriate to use it to compare safety performance between facilities or countries.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition: a scale for communicating the safety significance of radiation-related events to the public quickly and consistently; developed by the IAEA and OECD/NEA in 1990; current 2008 Edition User's Manual.
  2. Levels: 0 (below scale); 1–3 incidents (anomaly, incident, serious incident); 4–7 accidents (local and wider consequences, serious, major). One level ≈ 10× (logarithmic).
  3. Three assessment areas: ① people and environment (release, dose); ② radiological barriers and control (core damage, on-site contamination and dose rate); ③ defence in depth (degraded safety provisions).
  4. Final level: the highest of the three areas.
  5. Defence in depth capped at 3; additional factors (common-cause failure, procedures, safety culture) can add +1.
  6. Release criteria: I-131 equivalent TBq, boundaries about 500 / 5,000 / 50,000 TBq (Cs-137 × 40).
  7. Frequency categories: expected (> 10⁻²/yr), possible (10⁻⁴–10⁻²), unlikely (≤ 10⁻⁴), beyond design.
  8. South Korean procedure: licensee provisional level → NSSC Event Rating Committee final level; level 2 and above notified to the IAEA.
  9. Cases: Chernobyl 7, Fukushima 7 (provisional), Kyshtym 6, TMI-2 5 (core damage).
  10. Caution: separate from emergency classification; not for comparing safety between countries or facilities.

Exam relevance

INES has appeared five times in the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam: 76th exam, session 2, Q1 (INES classification criteria with representative accidents); 2010 exam, session 4, Q3 (INES initiating-event frequency categories — expected, possible, unlikely); 2012 exam, session 2, Q2 (the three assessment elements and the incident/accident split); 2012 exam, session 4, Q4 (the Shin-Kori Unit 1 coolant spray event and its rating); and 2019 exam, session 4, Q2 (event reporting methods and deadlines together with the rating system). Typical questions ask for the level structure with examples, the three areas and the highest-value rule, the frequency categories used in the defence-in-depth assessment, and how a specific operating event is reported and rated. Note that the 2010 question's "③ impossible event" is a mistranslation of the Manual's "Unlikely": it actually means an initiating event considered in the design but with a low likelihood (≤ 10⁻⁴/yr).
