Delayed Neutrons — Why 0.65 % of the Neutrons Makes a Reactor Controllable

Only 0.65 % of fission neutrons are delayed, but their precursor lifetimes stretch the mean generation time about 3,900 times and turn millisecond periods into controllable ones.

Concept C-RX-001
Definition

A delayed neutron is emitted when a fission-product precursor undergoes β⁻ decay and its daughter, left in an excited state above the neutron binding energy, immediately releases a neutron. The emission time is set by the precursor half-life (about 0.2–56 s), and in thermal fission of U-235 delayed neutrons make up about 0.65 % of all fission neutrons (β = 0.0065).

In short: neutrons released seconds to tens of seconds after fission via β⁻ decay of precursors; they lengthen the reactor's mean generation time and so make power control possible.

Principle and mechanism

How they differ from prompt neutrons

More than 99 % of fission neutrons are prompt neutrons, emitted within about 10⁻¹⁴ s of fission. Delayed neutrons come from some of the neutron-rich nuclides produced by fission. The classic example is the group-1 precursor Br-87. When Br-87 (half-life about 55.6 s) undergoes β⁻ decay, about 2.5 % of the time it produces a highly excited state of Kr-87, which immediately emits a neutron and becomes Kr-86. The rest decays through the Kr-87 ground state to Rb-87. In other words, the neutron emission itself is immediate; the "delay" comes from the β-decay half-life of the precursor.

The energies differ too. Prompt neutrons average about 2 MeV, while delayed neutrons are lower, about 0.25–0.6 MeV depending on the group. This difference is what gives rise to βeff.

The βᵢ sum to 0.0065, and the fraction-weighted mean precursor lifetime τ̄ = Σ(βᵢ/λᵢ)/β is about 13 s. One-group approximations commonly use λ ≈ 0.08 s⁻¹ (τ̄ ≈ 12.5 s).

Mean generation time and reactor period

The key effect of delayed neutrons is to lengthen the mean generation time. To the prompt neutron lifetime ℓp (slowing-down time + diffusion time), the mean precursor lifetime τ̄ is added for the fraction β.

ℓ̄ = (1−β)·ℓp + β·(ℓp + τ̄) ≈ ℓp + β·τ̄

As a worked example (slowing-down 10⁻⁶ s, diffusion 2×10⁻⁵ s, mean precursor lifetime 12.5 s), ℓp = 2.1×10⁻⁵ s and ℓ̄ ≈ 0.0813 s, about 3,900 times longer. The reason 0.65 % of the neutrons dominates the generation time is not the fraction itself but that the product β × τ̄ is overwhelmingly larger than ℓp.

For small reactivities the reactor period is approximately T ≈ ℓ̄/ρ. The exact relation comes from the inhour equation derived from the point kinetics equations, ρ = ωΛ + Σβᵢω/(ω+λᵢ) (ω = 1/T). β and βeff (summary)

βeff is the delayed neutron fraction weighted by importance (adjoint flux). Because delayed neutrons are born at lower energy, they leak out less while fast (higher importance), but they cannot cause fast fission of U-238 (threshold about 1 MeV) (lower importance). In small, highly enriched cores the leakage effect dominates, so βeff > β; as the reactor gets larger, the leakage advantage shrinks and βeff approaches β. In large low-enriched commercial PWRs the importance factor is about 0.97, slightly below 1.

What it means in a plant

  • Controllability: in the region ρ < β (delayed supercritical), periods are long — seconds to thousands of seconds — so control rod and boron adjustments fall within the response speed of operators and control systems. Startup-rate (SUR) limits also assume this region (1 dpm ≈ 26 s period). Operating limits on startup rate follow each plant's procedures.
  • The prompt-critical boundary: once ρ = β (one dollar) is reached, the reactor is critical on prompt neutrons alone and the period drops to the order of Λ/(ρ−β), i.e. milliseconds. At that point only prompt negative feedback such as the Doppler effect stops the power rise, so design and operation exclude this region.
  • Change with burnup: β of Pu-239 is about 0.0021, roughly one third of U-235 (0.0065). As the cycle proceeds, the share of Pu fission increases and βeff falls from about 0.006–0.007 at beginning of cycle to about 0.005 at end of cycle (core-specific; illustrative range). The same Δk then corresponds to more dollars, so reactivity accidents such as rod ejection are analysed conservatively with the end-of-cycle βeff. The β of U-238 fast fission (about 0.0148) raises the core-average β slightly.
  • Behaviour after shutdown: when a large negative reactivity is inserted, power drops promptly and then decreases with a period of about −80 s set by the mean lifetime of the group-1 precursor. There is no way to make it fall faster.
  • Reactor-type differences: in heavy-water reactors (CANDU), photoneutrons from (γ,n) reactions of high-energy gamma rays with deuterium act as additional delayed groups.

Regulatory and design criteria

The criteria below are examples from the United States (US NRC) and the IAEA.

  • US 10 CFR 50 Appendix A: GDC 11 (reactor inherent protection — designed so that prompt feedback suppresses power excursions), GDC 26 (two independent reactivity control systems), GDC 28 (reactivity limits: no damage to the reactor coolant pressure boundary or core cooling capability in reactivity accidents).
  • NUREG-0800 SRP 4.3 (Nuclear Design): reviews the calculation methods and uncertainties of kinetics parameters such as βeff and prompt neutron lifetime. SRP 15.4.8 (rod ejection) uses these parameters as analysis inputs.
  • Revision of reactivity-accident criteria: RG 1.77 (1974) was replaced in 2020 by RG 1.236, tightening fuel enthalpy and cladding failure criteria. Older statements of accident criteria should be read against the current criteria. The physics of delayed neutrons has not changed.
  • IAEA: the core design and reactivity control requirements of SSR-2/1 (Rev. 1, 2016) and SSG-52 (2019, core design) treat kinetics parameters as design considerations.

Key points at a glance

  1. Definition: neutrons emitted from the excited state of the daughter after β⁻ decay of fission-product precursors (Br-87, I-137, etc.).
  2. Cause of the delay: neutron emission is immediate; the delay comes from the precursor half-life (0.2–56 s).
  3. Fractions: U-235 β = 0.0065, Pu-239 about 0.0021, U-238 about 0.0148.
  4. Six groups: grouped by half-life; mean precursor lifetime about 13 s.
  5. βeff: importance-weighted fraction; lower energy → less leakage and less fast fission; large PWR about 0.97β.
  6. Mean generation time: ℓ̄ = ℓp + β·τ̄ increases from ≈ 2×10⁻⁵ s to about 0.08 s.
  7. Period: at ρ = 0.001, about 55 s with delayed neutrons versus 0.02 s with prompt neutrons only.
  8. Contribution to control: for ρ < β the period is long, so power can be controlled with control rods and boron.
  9. Prompt criticality: for ρ ≥ β (1$) the chain multiplies on prompt neutrons alone with periods of milliseconds, so it is excluded by design.
  10. Operational effects: βeff falls at end of cycle (Pu build-up) → applied conservatively in reactivity-accident analysis; after shutdown the period is about −80 s.
