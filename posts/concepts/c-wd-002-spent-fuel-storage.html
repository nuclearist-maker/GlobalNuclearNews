Spent Fuel Storage — Wet and Dry Storage, Interim Storage and Final Disposal

Keeping spent fuel cooled, shielded, subcritical and confined until it can be disposed of: wet pools, dry casks, interim storage and the deep repository.

Concept C-WD-002
Definition

Spent fuel storage is keeping spent fuel discharged from a reactor for a period of time with the intention of retrieving it. During this period decay heat is removed, shielding and criticality prevention are maintained, and radioactive materials are confined. Final disposal is isolation from the human environment with no intention of retrieval, and is therefore distinct from storage.

A good first line for an answer: "Spent fuel is kept until disposal while maintaining the cooling, shielding, criticality-prevention and confinement functions, and is managed in the order on-site wet and dry storage → interim storage → deep geological disposal."

Principle and mechanism

What time takes care of — decay heat and radioactivity

The storage method is determined by how decay heat decreases with time. Approximate values for PWR fuel (about 45 GWd/tHM) are:

  • about 10 kW/tHM one year after discharge
  • about 1.5–2 kW/tHM after 10 years
  • about 0.4–0.5 kW/tHM after 100 years

For up to several decades, Cs-137/Ba-137m and Sr-90/Y-90 (half-lives of about 30 years) contribute most; after that, Am-241 and Pu isotopes take a larger share. tHM refers to the initial heavy-metal mass.

Hot fuel just after discharge must be stored wet; once the heat has decreased enough, it is moved to dry storage cooled by natural air convection. The potential radiotoxicity is commonly said to fall to the level of the original natural uranium after several hundred years if only fission products are considered, and after about 100,000 years or more if actinides are included (depending on the basis of comparison). This is why the final step is deep geological disposal.

Composition of spent fuel

The approximate composition of 4 wt% enriched fuel burned for about four years (about 45 GWd/tHM) is as follows (2019 exam, session 3, Q3(a)).

Since 1 %FIMA ≈ 9.4 GWd/tHM, the fission-product fraction can be checked against the burnup.

Wet storage — the four safety functions of the pool

As in the 2008 exam, session 2, Q3, the functions are organized as nuclear, thermal-hydraulic and radiological aspects, plus confinement.

  • Nuclear safety (criticality prevention): US 10 CFR 50.68(b)(4) requires, without boron credit, keff ≤ 0.95 even if the pool is flooded with unborated water (95/95). If soluble boron credit is taken, keff ≤ 0.95 in borated water and keff < 1.0 in unborated water are required. The means are assembly spacing, neutron-absorber panels, borated water and burnup credit, and misloading accidents are also evaluated.
  • Thermal-hydraulic safety (decay heat removal): the pool cooling system (pumps, heat exchangers) limits the water temperature. The time to boiling after loss of cooling and the availability of makeup water are evaluated.
  • Radiological safety (shielding): several metres of water above the top of the fuel shield the gamma rays. The US Standard Technical Specifications require a water level of at least about 7 m (23 ft) above the top of the fuel during fuel handling.
  • Confinement and water chemistry: the purification system controls water quality, leakage is monitored, and failed fuel is stored in cans.

Expanding storage capacity and high-density racks

This is the 2010 exam, session 3, Q3 type. There are five ways to expand storage capacity:

  1. Replacing racks with high-density racks (re-racking)
  2. Region I/II storage using burnup credit
  3. Transfer between units
  4. Conversion to on-site dry storage
  5. Rod consolidation (demonstration level only)

High-density racks reduce assembly spacing and compensate for the added reactivity with neutron-absorber panels. The absorber needs:

  • adequate and uniform B-10 areal density (g/cm²)
  • stability under gamma irradiation
  • corrosion resistance in borated water
  • mechanical integrity and thermal conductivity
  • long-term monitorability using surveillance coupons

Boraflex (B₄C in a silicone polymer) has a history of gap formation and boron loss caused by gamma irradiation and silica dissolution (NRC GL 96-04), so Boral (B₄C–Al cermet), Metamic (B₄C–Al composite) and borated stainless steel are now mainly used. In South Korea, the storage racks of Kori Unit 2 are planned to be replaced with high-density racks.

Dry storage and transport casks

Dry storage places cooled fuel in a sealed canister backfilled with helium, inside a concrete or metal overpack, and cools it passively by natural air convection (2019 exam, session 3, Q3(c)). The key design limits are:

  • peak cladding temperature of 400 °C or less in normal storage (NRC NUREG-2215, formerly ISG-11 Rev. 3 — this is not the cask surface temperature)
  • double sealing
  • shielding to limit the dose at the site boundary
  • criticality prevention inside the cask

Types include metal dual-purpose casks (transport and storage), concrete canisters, modular vaults (MACSTOR) and vertical or horizontal concrete overpacks. The Wolsong pressurized heavy water reactor site is the representative South Korean case.

  • Canisters: 300 units (540 bundles each) were filled from 1992 to 2010.
  • MACSTOR: 7 modules (24,000 bundles each) have been in operation since 2010.
  • MACSTOR expansion: 7 more modules were completed in March 2022.

For light water reactor sites, the licensing review of a dry storage facility at the Hanbit site began in 2026. Kori aims to start operation in 2031.

Transport casks (2019 exam, session 3, Q3(b) and (d)) must pass the IAEA SSR-6 Type B(U)F accident-condition tests:

  • 9 m drop
  • puncture drop onto a steel bar from a height of 1 m
  • 800 °C fire for 30 minutes
  • immersion under 15 m of water for 8 hours (enhanced 200 m immersion for high-activity contents)

They also undergo normal-transport-condition tests and criticality evaluation. Fuel to be shipped to another plant is selected within the cask's licensed limits. The criteria are:

  • minimum cooling time
  • ranges of initial enrichment and burnup (loading curve)
  • decay heat per assembly and total heat load
  • neutron and gamma source-term limits
  • cladding integrity (failed fuel excluded or canned)
  • weight and geometry

Saturation is projected around 2030 for Hanbit, 2031 for Hanul and 2032 for Kori, with differences of one to three years between sources.

The "Special Act on the Management of High-Level Radioactive Waste" passed the National Assembly on 27 February 2025, was promulgated as Act No. 20843 on 25 March 2025 and took effect on 26 September 2025. The current version is Act No. 21738 (under the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment). Its main contents are:

  • Management committee: the High-Level Radioactive Waste Management Committee under the Prime Minister runs the program.
  • Site selection: exclusion of unsuitable areas → basic investigation → detailed investigation → local referendum.
  • Operating targets (Article 17(1)): an obligation to make efforts to start operating an interim storage facility before 2050 and a disposal facility before 2060.
  • Capacity of on-site storage facilities: limited to the amount projected to arise during the plants' design life.
  • Residents' views: residents' opinions must be gathered when planning an on-site storage facility.
  • Host-region support: special support funds and delivery fees.

The issue that limiting capacity to the design life conflicts with continued operation remains open.

Final disposal — deep geological disposal and KBS-3

High-level waste may not be disposed of near the surface. The standard concept is deep geological disposal, with multiple barriers in stable rock several hundred metres underground. The KBS-3 barrier system of Sweden and Finland is:

  1. Spent fuel
  2. Cast-iron insert
  3. Copper canister (about 5 cm)
  4. Bentonite buffer
  5. Backfill
  6. Crystalline bedrock (about 500 m at Forsmark, Sweden)

Progress abroad:

  • Onkalo, Finland: encapsulation trial operation was completed in 2025. In August 2026 STUK issued a positive safety assessment, and the government decision on the operating license is pending.
  • Forsmark, Sweden: construction began in January 2025.

Reprocessing and pyroprocessing are options to reduce volume and toxicity. Disposal of the high-level residues is still required.

Regulatory basis

  • Korea's Nuclear Safety Act: Article 2, item 18 (definition of radioactive waste — spent fuel is included only when it has been decided to dispose of it under Article 36-2), Enforcement Decree Article 2 (high-level threshold 2 kW/m³, 4,000 Bq/g). On-site dry storage requires a reactor operating license amendment; interim storage requires a construction and operating license for a radioactive waste management facility.
  • High-Level Radioactive Waste Act (enacted 2025, currently Act No. 21738, under the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment): definitions (storage, on-site storage facility, interim storage facility), committee, site selection, operating targets, capacity limit.
  • Korean technical standards: the fuel storage and handling provisions of Korea's Rule on Technical Standards for Reactor Facilities, etc. (Korean technical standard rule, Article 33; Article 61 for operation), KINS Safety Review Guidelines, and the NSSC Notice "Regulation on the Packaging and Transport of Radioactive Materials, etc.".
  • Overseas and IAEA: 10 CFR 50.68 and Part 72, NUREG-2215. IAEA SSG-15 Rev. 1 (2020, storage), SSR-5 (2011, disposal), SSR-6 Rev. 1 (2018, transport).
  • Changes since the questions were set: up to the 2023 and 2024 questions, the Special Act had not yet been enacted. Now the Special Act is in force and licensing of LWR dry storage has begun.

"Temporary storage" is not a current legal term in South Korea. In an answer it is safer to write "on-site storage (commonly called temporary storage)". Note, however, that the "on-site storage facility" of Article 2, item 7 of the Special Act excludes facilities installed under an operating license under Article 20 of the Nuclear Safety Act, so existing wet storage pools do not fall under this legal term.

Writing that spent fuel simply is high-level radioactive waste is also inaccurate. It is treated as radioactive waste only when it has been decided to dispose of it (Nuclear Safety Act Article 2, item 18; proviso to Article 2, item 2 of the Special Act); before that, it is managed as nuclear fuel material that has been used as reactor fuel.

Key points at a glance

  1. Temporary (on-site) storage: kept in the plant's wet pool → dry storage facility until delivery to an interim storage or disposal facility.
  2. Wet: borated-water pool, forced cooling, water shielding, keff ≤ 0.95 (unborated water assumed), high-density racks.
  3. Dry: sealed canister + He, passive cooling by natural air convection, peak cladding temperature ≤ 400 °C in normal storage, Wolsong canisters and MACSTOR.
  4. Interim storage: centralized off-site storage facility (wet or dry), retrieval assumed, several decades (Special Act target: operating before 2050).
  5. Final disposal: isolation with no intention of retrieval, deep geological disposal (several hundred metres underground), multiple barriers (KBS-3), target: operating before 2060.
  6. Safety functions: cooling, shielding, criticality prevention, confinement.
  7. Basis: High-Level Radioactive Waste Act (2025), Nuclear Safety Act, IAEA SSG-15 and SSR-5.

This is an example answer, not an official one, written to the current (2026) rules.

Exam relevance

In the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam spent fuel storage appeared six times between 2008 and 2024: 2008 exam, session 2, Q3 (nuclear, thermal-hydraulic and radiological safety in transfer and storage); 2010 exam, session 3, Q3 (expanding storage capacity and neutron absorbers for high-density racks); 2018 exam, session 4, Q4 (advantages and disadvantages of dry and wet storage); 2019 exam, session 3, Q3 (spent fuel composition, transport cask tests, dry storage and selection of fuel for loading); 2023 exam, session 1, Q6 (definitions and methods of temporary storage, interim storage and final disposal); and 2024 exam, session 4, Q2 (waste classification and temporary wet and dry storage of spent fuel). Most questions are descriptive comparisons. Separate storage (retrievable) from disposal (not retrievable), quote keff ≤ 0.95 with its unborated-water assumption, and note that "temporary storage" is not a current legal term.
