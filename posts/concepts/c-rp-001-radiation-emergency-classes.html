Radiation Protection & Emergency · C-RP-001

Radiation Emergency Classes (White, Blue, Red) — Three Classes by Facility State and Extent of Impact

Inside buildings, within the site or beyond the site: how the white, blue and red classes are defined, declared and linked to the PAZ and UPZ.

Concept C-RP-001
Definition

A radiation emergency is a situation in which radioactive material or radiation is released, or may be released, and urgent response is needed (Article 2 of Korea's Act on Physical Protection and Radiological Emergency, hereafter the Radiological Emergency Act). Article 17 of the same Act divides it, according to the severity and circumstances of the accident, into white, blue and red emergencies. Two criteria set the class: which barrier or safety function is damaged or threatened, and how far the radiological impact is expected to reach — inside buildings → within the site → beyond the site.

In an answer, one first line can carry both definition and criteria: "Classified under Article 17 by the severity and circumstances of the accident; the impact is expected to stay inside buildings (white), within the site (blue) or to reach beyond the site (red)."

Principle and mechanism

Legal definitions of the three classes

The criteria for each class are in [Annex 3] of the Enforcement Decree, "Criteria for the classes of radiation emergency and response procedures by class" (amended 25 October 2011), and each licensee's radiological emergency plan quotes the same wording.

ClassFacility state (damage or threat of damage)Expected extent of radiological impact
WhiteDamage to the sealing of radioactive material, or to the power supply function that maintains the safe stateInside the buildings of the nuclear facility
BlueDamage to major safety functions as recovery capability degrades from a white emergency, etc.Within the site of the nuclear facility
RedDamage to the last barrier through core damage or melting, etc.Beyond the site of the nuclear facility

Two points in these definitions must come through in an answer.

First, every class carries the phrase "has occurred or may occur". The class is declared on the expected extent of impact at the moment damage occurs or is threatened, without waiting for a measured release. A red emergency is declared not after radioactive material has left the site but when core damage and damage to the last barrier are threatened. That is what gives time to evacuate residents as a precaution before a release.

Second, the classes are tied to the state of the multiple barriers. One of the red-emergency conditions in the older emergency plans was "two of the three fission-product barriers (fuel cladding, reactor coolant system, containment) damaged and the remaining one at risk of damage".

The emergency action levels (EALs) are in the licensee's plan

The law sets only the definitions and the extent of impact. Which instrument readings and plant conditions trigger which class at each plant is set as emergency action levels (EALs) in the licensee's radiological emergency plan, which the NSSC approves (Article 20 of the Act). Typical conditions in plans of the 2000s (a PWR example):

  • White: severe fuel cladding damage (sharp rise in coolant I-131 dose-equivalent activity), rapid steam generator tube rupture (primary-to-secondary leakage of 120 gpm or more, or entry into the emergency operating procedure), reactor coolant leakage of 50 gpm or more, loss of all AC power, loss of essential DC power, a fire that can affect safety systems, an earthquake exceeding the operating basis earthquake
  • Blue: a loss-of-coolant accident beyond charging pump capacity, loss of all AC power lasting 15 minutes or more, loss of hot shutdown capability, inability to inject boron when the reactor cannot be shut down, major steam generator tube leakage during loss of offsite power
  • Red: expected site-boundary dose of 10 mSv/h whole body or 50 mSv/h thyroid or more; two of three barriers damaged + the third at risk; accidents threatening core melt and a serious release (loss of coolant + no safety injection + threatened containment integrity; loss of secondary heat removal + feed-and-bleed unavailable; total loss of AC power + turbine-driven auxiliary feedwater unavailable, etc.); seizure of vital plant areas

The same event can also be seen escalating over time. Loss of all AC power is white immediately and blue if it lasts 15 minutes or more. If auxiliary feedwater is also unavailable for 30 minutes or more so that core melt is threatened, it becomes red. Recent plans are moving toward matrix-type EALs organized by recognition category: radiation level, barrier status, hazards and system malfunctions.

What it means in a plant

  • Graded response (Annex 3): in a white emergency the licensee reports to the NSSC and others, discloses information, takes first-aid measures and operates its emergency response facilities; the NSSC notifies the relevant agencies. From blue, the licensee sets up its emergency headquarters and the NSSC sets up the central radiological emergency headquarters and the off-site emergency management center, which runs together with a joint information center. Red includes all blue actions, and in practice public protective actions such as precautionary evacuation of PAZ residents are carried out. Declaring a radiological disaster is a separate decision, taken at blue or above when the declaration criteria are met.
  • Target times: licensee plans aim to declare within 15 minutes, report to outside agencies within 15 minutes of declaration, and activate the emergency organization within 1 hour (plant plan example). The Act itself sets only the duty to report according to the plan's procedures (Article 21).
  • Link to the planning zones: under the amended Act in force from 22 November 2014 (Article 20-2; zones reset site by site in 2015), the radiological emergency planning zone of power reactors was split into a precautionary action zone (PAZ) of 3–5 km radius and an urgent protective action planning zone (UPZ) of 20–30 km radius. Before the amendment it was a single zone of 8–10 km radius. In a red emergency the PAZ gets precautionary evacuation and similar actions before a release; in the UPZ, sheltering, evacuation and thyroid-blocking agents follow dose assessment, environmental monitoring and the off-site center's decision. The 3–5 km and 20–30 km are setting ranges for each zone's outer radius, not the distance radiation reaches.
  • Termination: when the plant condition and environmental dose rates stabilize below the white declaration criteria, the emergency is terminated in consultation with the off-site emergency management center and others.

Regulatory basis

  • Radiological Emergency Act (enacted May 2003, in force February 2004; current Act No. 20720, in force 1 January 2026): Article 17 (classes), Article 20 (approval of the radiological emergency plan), Article 20-2 (emergency planning zones), Article 21 (licensee reporting duty), Article 23 (declaration of a radiological disaster)
  • Enforcement Decree: Article 19 and [Annex 3] (criteria and response procedures by class); criteria for declaring a radiological disaster. Under the current Article 25 of the Decree, the criterion is 10 mSv/h whole body or 50 mSv/h thyroid or more at the site boundary (the same as the 2013 Decree).
  • NSSC Notice "Radiological emergency measures of nuclear licensees": detailed criteria for preparing emergency plans
  • IAEA GSR Part 7 (2015): classification system of general emergency / site area emergency / facility emergency / alert
  • US 10 CFR 50.47(b)(4) and Appendix E, NUREG-0654, NEI 99-01: four classes — Unusual Event / Alert / Site Area / General Emergency. State and local governments are notified within 15 minutes of declaring a class.
  • Changes since the questions were set: the Ministry of Education, Science and Technology in the 2011 questions became the NSSC (October 2011). The planning zones became the PAZ/UPZ system (Article 20-2, added 21 May 2014, in force 22 November 2014, zones reset in May 2015). The 2016 national implementation plan considered renaming the classes facility/site/area emergency and unifying them with the disaster-management crisis alert levels, but white/blue/red were still used up to the 2023 exam. Its proposal to start the central headquarters at a blue emergency was made law by the Decree amendment of 23 December 2021 (Presidential Decree No. 32246): what was item (d) of the disaster-declaration actions in Annex 3 is now an independent item (item 3) of the NSSC's blue-emergency actions.

Key points at a glance

10-point model answer: classes of radiation emergency

  1. Basis: Radiological Emergency Act Article 17 and Decree Annex; three classes by the severity and circumstances of the accident.
  2. White emergency: damage (or threat) to the sealing of radioactive material or to the power supply function for the safe state; impact expected to stay inside buildings.
  3. Blue emergency: damage (or threat) to major safety functions through degraded recovery capability; impact expected to stay within the site.
  4. Red emergency: damage (or threat) to the last barrier through core damage or melting; impact expected beyond the site.
  5. Declaration principle: declared on the expected extent of impact when damage occurs or is threatened → time for precautionary protective actions before a release.
  6. Declaration criteria: plant-specific EALs set in the radiological emergency plan and approved by the NSSC.
  7. Target times (example): declaration within 15 minutes, outside report within 15 minutes of declaration, emergency organization activated within 1 hour.
  8. Public protection in red: precautionary evacuation etc. in the PAZ (3–5 km); in the UPZ (20–30 km) sheltering, evacuation and thyroid-blocking agents decided from assessment and monitoring results.
  9. Distinction from a radiological disaster: a disaster is declared by the NSSC on the Article 23 criteria; red ≠ automatic declaration.
  10. International equivalents: white ≈ Alert, blue ≈ Site Area, red ≈ General Emergency.

This is a model answer, not an official solution. It reflects the criteria current as of 2026.

Exam relevance

Radiation emergency classes are among the most frequent topics in the Korean Professional Engineer (Nuclear Power) exam, with ten appearances: 2009 exam, session 2, Q1; 2010 exam, session 2, Q2; 2011 exam, session 2, Q3 and Q5; 2014 exam, session 1, Q1; 2017 exam, session 1, Q13; 2018 exam, session 1, Q12; 2019 exam, session 3, Q5; 2022 exam, session 2, Q4; and 2023 exam, session 4, Q4. They ask for the classes and definitions (often with five or more declaration conditions per class), red-emergency events "threatening core melt together with a serious release" (2010, 2011), organizational responses in white and red emergencies (2011, 2019), radiation emergency versus radiological disaster and the declaration criteria (2018, 2023), urgent protective action criteria (2017) and SG tube leakage conditions per class (2022). Write the class definitions as "facility state + expected extent of impact", stress "occurs or is threatened", and keep red emergency and disaster declaration separate.
