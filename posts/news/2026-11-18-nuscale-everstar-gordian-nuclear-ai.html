What AI Speeds Up in Nuclear Paperwork: NuScale's Gordian Rollout and DOE's 208-Page, One-Day Demonstration

NuScale is extending Everstar's Gordian AI platform from an initial pilot to six business areas, and a DOE demonstration produced a 208-page licensing-format document in one day for expert review.

2026.11.18Includes concept images

Building and operating a nuclear plant runs on documents: licensing applications, design change reviews, quality assurance records and supply chain paperwork. When a company says it is putting artificial intelligence (AI) to work on that load, two questions follow naturally. What actually gets faster? And who keeps the results accurate? Two recent announcements give us concrete material to work with, along with a few headline numbers that are worth reading carefully.

NuScale's AI Rollout: From Pilot to Six Business Areas

On November 18, 2026, NuScale Power announced that it is expanding its use of Gordian, a nuclear-specific AI platform from New York-based Everstar, beyond an initial pilot into six business areas: licensing, plant services, project management, supply chain, quality assurance and product development.

According to NuScale, Gordian applies nuclear regulatory frameworks across a connected set of documents so that users can assess the potential impact of a design change. It pairs workflows for repetitive tasks with a natural-language interface that operates under nuclear-industry guidelines. The stated goal is to lighten the documentation burden so that engineers can apply their expertise to more projects.

The current stage is clear: the pilot showed efficiency gains, and NuScale has now widened the scope. Specific figures for time or cost savings have not been published, and the two companies are evaluating further areas of application.

Placing the known Gordian deployments in sequence shows how the use has grown:

  • NANO Nuclear Energy (November 26, 2024): completed a three-month regulatory and licensing pilot, reporting that better aggregation of regulatory information supported the development of documents for NRC submission.
  • US Department of Energy (March 26, 2026): a demonstration in which a 208-page document was generated in one day and then evaluated by experts.
  • NuScale (November 18, 2026): expansion from a pilot to six business areas, with quantitative results yet to be released.

The pattern moves from a single-task pilot, to a structured government demonstration, to a tool used across an organization's departments.

208 Pages in One Day: Reading the Conditions and Numbers Behind DOE's Demonstration

The DOE demonstration brought together Idaho National Laboratory (INL), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), Microsoft and Everstar. The input was a Preliminary Documented Safety Analysis (PDSA) for a generic High Temperature Gas Reactor (HTGR) from the National Reactor Innovation Center (NRIC). The task was to carry the content of this DOE-format safety document into the section structure of an NRC application. The method, called semantic ontology mapping, matches concepts and data relationships between two different documentation systems. Think of it as a translation table between two agencies' tables of contents and vocabularies.

DOE reported that the same drafting task had previously taken four to six weeks, while the AI generated the document in one day. Experts then evaluated the 208-page output for accuracy, omissions, consistency, grammar and structure. DOE plans to develop a follow-on review agent and an evaluation framework for grading performance. According to Microsoft, the document was written for the demonstration and was not submitted to the NRC. Effects on regulatory review time, the number of follow-up questions and licensing outcomes will be measurable once real submissions and review histories exist.

Now the arithmetic. Four to six weeks is 28 to 42 calendar days, so by elapsed time the AI step took roughly 1/28 to 1/42 of the earlier duration (calculated: 1 ÷ 28 and 1 ÷ 42).

The Gordian product page describes the same type of case as going "from 200 person-days to 1 day" and shows a figure of 99.5%. The calculation matches: 1 ÷ 200 = 0.5%, so the reduction is 100% − 0.5% = 99.5%.

The units deserve a closer look. A person-day measures labor, the combined effort of everyone involved. A day measures elapsed calendar time. A true savings rate comes from putting both sides in the same unit, which means counting the people who ran the AI process and the hours experts spent reviewing the output alongside the original drafting effort.

How AI Handles Nuclear Documents: Search, Evidence and Knowledge Graphs

Microsoft's October 2026 customer story describes how Gordian works under the hood. On Azure, Airflow orchestrates the data pipeline, and complex documents are converted into tables, semantically chunked text, tagged images and a knowledge graph. Azure AI Search and Azure Document Intelligence handle search and document interpretation, and text generation draws on Anthropic's Claude and Azure OpenAI models through Microsoft Foundry.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) works much like an open-book exam. Before writing an answer, the system first finds the relevant source passages, lays them out, and builds its response on top of them. A knowledge graph is a relationship map linking equipment, documents and requirements. Everstar describes detecting equipment tags on drawings and connecting them, so that when one valve changes, a reviewer can trace which upstream and downstream equipment, and which documents, are affected.

Sources and work history matter especially in nuclear documentation. A single document title often exists in several revisions, and every review starts from knowing exactly which revision, and which location in it, supports a given statement. When the AI records its citation locations, a reviewer can check them against the original right away. Final technical determinations and sign-off remain with designated responsible engineers. An earlier post on Euratom's use of AI for safeguards surveillance footage showed the same arrangement, with inspectors making the final call.

Performance Figures, Security Conditions and What to Watch Next

Microsoft's case study cites drawing detection accuracy of more than 96%. That figure describes the step of finding equipment tags on drawings. The accuracy of inferring relationships between components and of supporting design judgments is a separate quantity, measured on its own.

On security, several items are public. Everstar's Azure announcement describes per-customer isolation, access management, audit logging and data residency controls. The product page lists SOC 2, FedRAMP readiness, on-premises deployment and 10 CFR 810 compliance. 10 CFR Part 810 is the US regulation governing transfers of unclassified nuclear technology and assistance related to foreign nuclear activities, and it covers electronic documents and knowledge transfer as well. Everstar states that customer data is kept out of model training. Per-user pricing and fee structures were not found in the public materials.

Everstar launched Gordian Research in September 2025, built around source-linked question answering and searches of regulatory precedent. In March 2026 it announced a plan to expand step by step from licensing into design and manufacturing. Work planning, operator training and quality assurance document automation are currently marked "Coming Soon."

The next checkpoints are NuScale's quantitative results from the pilot and expansion, and DOE's planned follow-on review agent and performance grading framework.

AI shortens the time spent finding material and producing first drafts, while experts carry the work of weighing evidence and approving results. When the next set of savings figures appears, a useful first question is whether it measures elapsed time or labor.
